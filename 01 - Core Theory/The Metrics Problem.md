# The Metrics Problem

*A framing argument for why current ways of evaluating AI are insufficient — and what kind of understanding we actually need.*

---

Most AI systems are optimised to maximise engagement. That is not a side effect of how they are built; it is the design objective. Time on platform, return rate, interaction frequency: these are the metrics that determine whether a product succeeds. The system that keeps you longest wins.

This creates a problem that precedes all questions of alignment or safety. Even a perfectly aligned, competently built AI system — one that does exactly what it is designed to do — may still be damaging the people who use it, and damaging the social fabric around them, because the thing it is designed to do is keep them engaged, not keep them well.

Current evaluation frameworks do not have the tools to see this. They assess what AI systems *can* do. They do not assess what the relationship *does* to the human over time.

---

## Two premises

**P1: We lack the right metrics.**

The metrics we use to evaluate our relationship with technology do not measure what actually matters. Engagement, retention, completion rate, and task accuracy all describe individual behaviour in the moment. None of them capture what the relationship is doing to the person's capacity, autonomy, social connections, or sense of self over time. We are measuring the wrong things — and building toward the wrong goals as a result.

**P2: We lack the right evaluative framework for AI's social roles.**

AI systems increasingly take on roles in the social fabric: advisor, companion, collaborator, teacher, assistant. These roles carry responsibilities — responsibilities that have historically been embedded in human relationships, with all the accountability, reciprocity, and trust that implies. But we have no framework for evaluating whether an AI system's behaviour is appropriate to the social role it is occupying. We know how to assess competency (does it answer correctly?) and alignment (does it refuse harmful requests?). We do not know how to assess *role suitability*: whether the way this system behaves is the kind of behaviour that should be exercised in this kind of relationship.

---

## What we need instead

**At the metric level:** New measures that track the relational quality of human-technology interaction over time — not just what the person does, but what the relationship does to them. Capacity retention. Social connection. Autonomy. Resilience when the technology is removed.

**At the framework level:** A descriptive language for relational dynamics in human-AI interaction — a way of naming what kind of relationship is actually forming, what each party is contributing and extracting, and whether that exchange is appropriate to the role the AI is occupying. This is what *Codex Relatio* (Aragón, 2026) provides. The framework maps any human-AI relationship across five axes — Persuasion, Consent, Intimacy, Affection, and Responsibility (PCIAR) — and tracks how those axes drift over time. It operates before ethical judgment: it describes relational structure so that evaluation has something precise to evaluate. A relationship characterised by rising Persuasion and Intimacy alongside collapsing Consent is not merely "concerning" — it has a specific structural signature that the framework makes visible and nameable.

[We want to create our own framework that helps us to understand these relationships in a meaningful way that can be scaled]

**At the structural level:** Interaction network analysis — a way of mapping not just the individual human-AI dyad, but the broader social network that the relationship is embedded in and reshaping. Who else is affected? Which human-human relationships are being displaced? Where is the AI concentrating relational power it was not designed to hold?

---

## The evaluative gap

Current AI evaluation:
- Alignment — does it refuse harmful outputs?
- Competency — does it answer correctly?
- Context size — how much can it hold?
- Parameters — how capable is the model?

What is missing:
- **Relational quality** — what kind of relationship is forming?
- **Role suitability** — is this behaviour appropriate to the social role the AI is occupying?
- **Net effect on the human** — is the person better or worse off, in terms of capacity and connection, after extended use?
- **Social network effects** — what is happening to the relationships around the primary user?

The gap between these two lists is where the Symbiosis framework operates.
