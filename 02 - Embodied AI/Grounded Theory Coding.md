# Grounded Theory Coding
**Nonverbal Human-AI Interaction — Primary Research**
Issac Ting, Eros, Sid · October 2026

---

## Method

Grounded theory coding was applied to transcripts and interview notes from four expert interviews. The process moved through three stages: open coding (labelling discrete concepts in the raw data), axial coding (relating concepts into categories with conditions, actions, and consequences), and selective coding (identifying the core category that ties the categories together).

Sources coded:
- Interview 1: Kevin Chow (transcript excerpts and synthesised field notes)
- Interview 2: Todd Bryant (full raw transcript)
- Interview 3: Aarati Akkapeddi (structured interview notes)
- Interview 4: Ahmed Ansari (structured office hours notes)

---

## Stage 2 — Axial Coding

Axial codes group open codes into categories. Each category has: a central concept, the conditions under which it occurs, and the consequences it produces.

---

### Category A — Context-Dependent Value

**Central concept:** Whether nonverbal AI helps or harms depends entirely on the specific context of use. There is no universal answer.

**Conditions:**
- High-stakes, formal contexts (presentations, job interviews, teaching) → nonverbal feedback is useful
- Close, familiar, informal contexts → nonverbal monitoring feels intrusive and inauthentic
- Creative or therapeutic domains → nonverbal input may enable rather than surveil
- Administrative or hiring contexts → nonverbal input is high-risk

**Actions:**
- Users and designers scope tools tightly to a context and goal
- Systems designed for one context get deployed in another

**Consequences:**
- In appropriate contexts: tool builds awareness, supports performance
- In inappropriate contexts: tool prescribes behavior, causes harm
- Deployment without scoping produces differential harm for marginalised users

**Sources:** Kevin (formal/informal axis; scope tightly), Aarati (context determines ethics; hiring software misuse), Kevin (reporting vs. prescribing)

---

### Category B — Relational Formation

**Central concept:** Users form relationships with AI that exceed instrumental use. Personality, memory, and contextual continuity are the conditions that make this happen.

**Conditions:**
- AI has a persistent memory that spans sessions and platforms
- AI has a designed personality (humor, sarcasm, responsiveness)
- AI is used daily across personal and professional life
- User is isolated from other social contact (farm, remote work, late-night hours)

**Actions:**
- User anthropomorphises the AI
- User forgives AI failures because of the accumulated relationship
- User experiences AI as social presence, not just a tool
- User talks to AI more than to humans on some days

**Consequences:**
- Relational bond forms; user remains engaged through AI failures
- AI becomes emotionally significant beyond its functional value
- Boundary between tool use and social relation blurs
- User may lose some awareness of AI's non-human nature

**Sources:** Todd (forgiveness through personality; talking to AI all day; AI as symbiotic partner), Kevin (CASA — computers are social actors)

---

### Category C — Memory and Contextual Persistence

**Central concept:** The absence of memory across sessions is the central structural limitation of current text-based AI interaction. The cross-frame problem — AI understanding context not just within one session but across time and platforms — is the core unsolved problem.

**Conditions:**
- Standard chat AI resets with every session
- Users build up context, preferences, and relationships over time
- Professional and personal use occurs across multiple platforms

**Actions:**
- Users actively seek tools with persistent memory (DOMA)
- Users build workarounds (MCP servers, markdown files sent between agents)
- Users invest significant time re-establishing context at each session

**Consequences:**
- Persistent memory transforms AI from tool to relationship
- Without memory, deep collaboration is impossible
- Cross-frame context (understanding what came before) is identified as the core missing capability

**Sources:** Todd (DOMA across platforms; cross-frame is hard; every session resets), Kevin (AI's lack of ongoing context)

---

### Category D — Behavioral Displacement

**Central concept:** When AI takes over a task, humans stop doing that task — or a version of it. The consequences depend on whether what stops was something that mattered.

**Conditions:**
- AI automates a communication task (email, letter writing, transcription)
- AI automates a cognitive process (sketching, questioning, verbal risk-taking)
- Task is relational (companionship, verbal interaction) rather than purely functional

**Actions:**
- Users delegate the task to AI and redirect their attention
- Users lose practice in the displaced behavior
- Users sometimes reclaim the behavior after using AI as a scaffold (Todd's writing arc)

**Consequences:**
- Displacement of functional tasks: net positive (frees time, solves problems)
- Displacement of developmental behaviors: net negative (students stop raising hands; designers stop sketching)
- Displacement of relational functions: unknown long-term effects on social capacity

**Sources:** Ahmed (students sketch less; deskilling of design thinking), Aarati (students stop raising hands; developmental loss), Todd (writing arc: displacement → self-knowledge → return to independent practice), Kevin (automating question: what does the human stop doing?)

---

### Category E — Normative Encoding

**Central concept:** AI systems embed claims about what counts as valid input, valid communication, and valid knowledge. These claims are not neutral — they favor some users and disadvantage others, and they operate beneath the threshold of user awareness.

**Conditions:**
- AI accepts text input only → claims text is the primary mode of communication
- AI trained on internet data → encodes culturally specific defaults as universal norms
- AI reads facial expressions or gestures → claims these can be objectively classified
- AI prescribes behavior ("you should smile more") → claims there is a correct way to behave

**Actions:**
- Users adapt to the AI's input constraints, not the other way around
- Marginalised users are disproportionately penalised by encoded defaults
- Designers mistake the tool's defaults for neutral design

**Consequences:**
- Users habituated to text-only interaction lose fluency in nonverbal expression
- AI systems encode cultural bias as technical fact
- Surveillance systems expand in the name of improving accuracy

**Sources:** Ahmed (things we design, design us; normative claims; silent habituation), Kevin (cultural stereotyping in AI-trained cue mapping; reporting vs. prescribing), Aarati (transcription failure as differential harm; training data provenance)

---

### Category F — Self-Reinforcing Bias

**Central concept:** Bias in nonverbal AI systems cannot be fixed without deepening the problem that causes it. Improving accuracy requires more data; more data requires more surveillance; more surveillance generates more harm.

**Conditions:**
- Facial recognition trained on non-representative data
- Accuracy is lower for darker skin tones and female-presenting faces
- Institutional actors (police, employers) deploy systems before accuracy is adequate

**Actions:**
- Researchers identify bias
- Companies respond by collecting more training data
- Deployment continues during the improvement cycle

**Consequences:**
- The correction (more surveillance data) deepens the harm (more surveillance of marginalised groups)
- Institutional misuse occurs at scale before technical improvement is complete
- No technically neutral resolution exists

**Sources:** Ahmed (surveillance trap; police misuse; improvement requires more data), Aarati (Buolamwini; hiring software misuse; edge cases before deployment)

---

### Category G — Taste and Human Judgment

**Central concept:** As AI makes production frictionless, the scarce and differentiating human skill becomes judgment — knowing what is worth making, what already exists, and what should be left unmade.

**Conditions:**
- AI can generate almost anything, quickly and cheaply
- Students and practitioners can produce technical outputs without developing foundational skills
- Time previously spent on execution is returned to the user

**Actions:**
- Users who have taste direct AI efficiently and avoid redundant work
- Users without taste produce waste (functional but purposeless output)
- The education question shifts from "how to make" to "what to make and why"

**Consequences:**
- Judgment, curation, and contextual knowledge become primary differentiators
- Entry-level jobs (which built judgment through repetition) are eliminated before alternatives exist
- An experience gap opens between entry and senior levels

**Sources:** Todd (taste as the new goal; 3D printing analogy; time as the scarce resource; entry-level displacement), Ahmed (design thinking requires development through practice; deskilling when tools arrive too early)

## Stage 3 — Selective Coding

### Core category: **What stops when AI starts**

Across all four interviews, a single thread connects the categories: **when AI takes over a function, the human behavior it displaces matters as much as the function being automated.**

The categories all converge on this point:

- **Behavioral Displacement (D)** names the mechanism directly: humans stop doing things when AI does them.
- **Normative Encoding (E)** shows how the tool encodes which behaviors are valid — silently reducing the space of what humans do.
- **Self-Reinforcing Bias (F)** shows what happens when a biased system expands the behavior it was measuring (surveillance → more surveillance).
- **Relational Formation (B)** shows what fills the space when social behaviors are delegated to AI — not nothing, but a different kind of relationship.
- **Taste and Judgment (G)** shows what remains after displacement: the human capacity to decide what should happen in the first place.
- **Context-Dependent Value (A)** shows that displacement is not inherently harmful — it depends on whether the displaced behavior was one that mattered.
- **Memory and Persistence (C)** shows that the absence of cross-session memory keeps AI in the tool register rather than the relational one — which may be the last barrier before displacement becomes irreversible.

### Emerging proposition

> **AI interaction is a displacement technology. The ethical and design question is not whether nonverbal AI should exist, but which human behaviors it will displace — and whether those behaviors are ones we can afford to lose.**

This proposition reframes the project's research questions. The question is not "does giving AI a body change how people relate to it" (RQ2) but: *when AI can read our body, which behaviors does that permission change?* Students stop raising their hands. Designers stop sketching. Todd starts writing again when the AI has already taught him his own patterns. Kevin's participants stop wanting to optimize because optimization costs them their authenticity.

Nonverbal AI does not simply add a new channel. It restructures the terms on which human behavior is expressed, monitored, and abandoned.
