# Primary Research Documentation
**Nonverbal Human-AI Interaction — Pre-Thesis Project**
Issac Ting · October 2026

---

## 1. Research Overview

This project explores how people interact with AI today, and what might change if AI could understand more than just text. Almost all human-AI interaction currently happens through typing or speech that gets converted to text. But people communicate in many other ways — through gesture, expression, sketching, tone of voice, and physical presence. This research examines what gets lost in text-only interaction, and what opening up nonverbal channels might mean for how people relate to AI.

**Research Questions**

- **RQ1:** How are people using AI today, and what forms does it take in their lives?
- **RQ2:** How does giving AI a physical or virtual body change the way people relate to it?
- **RQ3:** Does the type of body AI has affect how much people trust it, feel connected to it, or follow its guidance?

## 2. Interview Guide and Protocol

### Research briefing (read to all participants at the start of each interview)

Thank you for taking the time to speak with us. Before we begin, we'd like to give you some context on what we're working on.

The three of us are working together on our pre-thesis research, and our topic is nonverbal human-AI interaction. Today, almost all of our communication with AI happens through text or speech. Even when we speak to it through dictation tools, our words are still turned into text before the AI responds. But that's not how people naturally communicate with each other. When we explain an idea, we point, sketch, gesture, show objects, and use facial expressions. A lot of meaning lives outside of words.

We're interested in what it would mean for AI to understand us in those ways too — through drawing, scribbling, gesturing, showing things, and expressing ourselves, without having to translate every thought into text first.

We're at an early, exploratory stage. The three of us will research this space together first, and then each of us will carve out our own direction for our individual theses. That's why we're speaking with experts like you. We're not looking for feedback on a specific study. We'd like to hear how you think about this subject, drawing on your own experience and expertise, so we can understand it more deeply and find directions we may not have considered.

There are no right or wrong answers. Please feel free to take any question wherever you find it most interesting, share stories or examples, or tell us if you think we're framing something the wrong way.

Would it be all right if we recorded this conversation or took notes, just for our own research purposes?

---

### Core 5 questions

1. Can you tell us a bit about your work or practice?
2. What have you noticed about how AI shapes the way people behave or communicate?
3. Tell me about a time AI completely misunderstood you. What got lost?
4. If AI could understand your nonverbal communication — gestures, sketches, expressions — what would you use it for?
5. How would you feel if an AI could read your facial expressions, emotions, and tone of voice? What excites or concerns you about that?

---

### Follow-up prompts

1. So much of how we communicate is nonverbal — pointing, sketching on a napkin, a raised eyebrow. Why do you think our interaction with AI has been reduced almost entirely to text?
2. When you explain an idea to a colleague or student, how much of it happens through gesture, drawing, or showing? Can you recall a time when words alone weren't enough?
3. What gets lost when we have to translate a thought into text before an AI can understand it?
4. If you could point, scribble, or gesture at an AI and it truly understood you, what's the first thing you would use it for?
5. What would it mean for an AI to "understand" a gesture or a sketch? Is that the same as understanding a sentence?
6. Should nonverbal input replace text and speech, or work alongside them? What might that combination look like?
7. Should the AI respond nonverbally too — by sketching, showing, or reacting? Or does that feel like a step too far?
8. Right now AI lives inside a screen. Nonverbal interaction would require AI to "see" us. How might it feel to be watched by the thing you're talking to?
9. Many gestures and expressions are culturally specific. Whose nonverbal language would AI end up learning by default, and who might be left out?
10. Are there historical or non-Western ideas about communication — craft traditions, ritual, tacit knowledge — that could change how we design AI for nonverbal interaction?

## 3. Interviews and Transcripts

### Interview 1 — Kevin Chow
**Date:** September 25, 2026
**Participant:** Kevin Chow, PhD researcher in human-computer interaction
**How contacted:** Personal connection through Issac (a mutual colleague, Sid, made the introduction)
**Format:** Remote video call. Full transcript in accompanying PDF: *Meeting Title: Issac <> Kevin.pdf*

**Context:** Kevin's PhD research included a study in which a computer vision system tracked nonverbal cues — smiling frequency, nodding rate, posture — in real work video meetings. The study was inspired by Zoom's move toward emotion AI during the pandemic. This made him an expert witness on both the technical challenges of reading nonverbal behavior and the human experience of being monitored in that way.

**Key excerpts from transcript:**

> "We explored more specifically looking at cues instead of emotions. At a much lower level, looking at things like smiling or nodding — because those are more objective things. I can actually capture and see how many times someone is smiling without necessarily saying something about what that means."

> "People found that there were a lot of times where they didn't care to have ideal or good nonverbal cues. Imperfect nonverbal cues — maybe I'm not smiling a lot, or I look really tired — especially in meetings where you were close with your colleagues, those were actually seen as being really important. They felt more authentic. The imperfection is what makes it human."

> "In the design of the system, we were very careful to keep it neutral. One thing we could have done is say 'you should smile more.' Instead, we said 'you haven't smiled in the last two minutes.' These are your metrics. Do what you want with them."

> "I would say scope it tightly to a specific context and specific goal. In general, you wouldn't be able to say much — I would say a lot of the time I probably don't need it. In some contexts, it could be very, very useful, but it's still difficult to decide how to act on it."

> "Paralanguage is the non-word aspects of your voice that communicate meaning — your tone, emotional quality, pitch, speaking rate, pauses and silences. It would be very interesting. I don't know if there's too much research on that. I'm not sure whether voice AI uses this or just does speech to text and only looks at the text."

### Interview 3 — Aarati Akkapeddi
**Date:** September 23, 2026
**Participant:** Aarati Akkapeddi, artist and technologist; faculty
**How contacted:** NYU faculty contact through Sid
**Format:** In-person interview. Notes in accompanying file: *Interview with Aarati Akkapeddi.md*

**Context:** Akkapeddi works across coding, performance, and visual design, with a research focus on AI, text and verbal communication, and handmade datasets. She brought both practitioner and educator perspectives — she teaches a Research Methods course in which students experienced AI transcription failure firsthand, and her artistic practice includes designing systems for live nonverbal interaction.

**Key themes from notes:**

- LLM mediation of everyday exchanges (email, text) is now common; over-reliance in pedagogy is a growing concern
- K-12 teachers report students preferring to ask LLMs rather than raising their hands in class, chilling the development of bravery, questioning, and verbal interaction
- In her Research Methods course, remote students used auto-transcription for recorded lectures; transcript quality was poor — "chocolate wrong" — and mistranslated terms risked compounding confusion for students already disadvantaged by time zone differences
- Gesture-based AI has genuine potential for performance art (real-time interaction with responsive systems) and disability justice (expanded communication options)
- More concerned than excited overall: sentiment analysis and facial recognition have long histories of bias; Joy Buolamwini's research on facial recognition, gender, and race is cited as foundational
- Hiring software that uses video interview analysis is a concrete current misuse case
- Context is critical — the application determines whether excitement or caution is warranted; edge cases and inaccuracy must be addressed before deployment
- Open model weights are preferable to closed, but transparency on weights does not resolve bias in training data; training data provenance is rarely disclosed even with open weights

### Interview 4 — Ahmed Ansari (Office Hours)
**Date:** September 23, 2026
**Participant:** Ahmed Ansari (aa7703@nyu.edu), PhD in design studies; faculty
**How contacted:** NYU faculty office hours, attended by Sid
**Format:** Office hours conversation. Notes in accompanying file: *Interview with Ahmed Ansari.md*

**Context:** Ansari's PhD research focuses on the intellectual and conceptual history of design — how ideas about research and design shift over time. His core framework, drawn from Tony Fry and Heidegger, holds that "the things we design, design us": designed objects embed ontological and normative claims that shape behavior silently. He does not use AI for his own research (citing unreliable citations and misrepresentation of literature), but uses it as a creative tool in studio and game design courses.

**Key themes from notes:**

- Technologies embed normative claims about how the world is and should be; AI changes practices and habituates users faster than they notice, with deeper effects on cognition, memory, empathy, and theory of mind
- Affective computing and facial recognition raise significant bias issues: accuracy drops for darker skin tones and female-presenting faces; misidentification is used by police and government agencies and disproportionately harms people of color and women; improving accuracy requires more surveillance data, which creates its own ethical problem with no clean exit
- Sketching and drawing as nonverbal creative process: Malcolm McCullough (*Abstracting Craft*) and Gabriella Goldschmidt on move-making and reflective practice; CAD tools changed but did not eliminate craft; AI poses a similar but faster disruption
- Students are sketching less and moving to digital tools too early; this undermines the development of design thinking
- UX as a profession is only about 20 years old with no established benchmarks or standards; AI has accelerated role collapse (designers now expected to do research, development, strategy, and management), but the field's identity crisis predates AI by 25 years: "the field is an absolute disaster and we're in a world of trouble"

## 4. Field Notes

Our research interest within the group converged towards a shared interest in how we interact with AI, and also exploring the possible ways we can expand beyond current modes of interactions. Where my interests diverge between the other two team members was I was less focused on the actual interaction and more interested in the social dynamics that shifts as a result of those interactions. So my secondary research as well as coding deviates from the interaction and modality layer and looks a bit more at the underlying cognitive behaviour and socialogical factors.
 
**How does conversation shape the way we understand the world?**
- People will find ways to connect — the drive toward connection is fundamental
- Observing → generating insight → people are lonely → we will go to great lengths to avoid loneliness

Insight 01 
**On digital adoption**
- Not all users adapt to new technology at the same rate
- Multiple chat platforms: what does it mean for how people relate to AI when they have different relationships with different systems?

**Two axes that emerged from discussion**

```
Instrumental ←————————————→ Relational
                    ↕
         Prescribed behavior
                    ↕
         Emergent behavior
```

- **Instrumental** — using AI as a tool to get something done
- **Relational** — relating to AI as an entity
- **Prescribed** — the interface tells you how to behave
- **Emergent** — behavior arises unpredictably from the interaction

**The core problem with AI:** it looks like a tool (chat interface, prescribed inputs) but activates relational dynamics (emergent behavior). The interface is instrumental. The effect is relational. That gap is where harm hides.

**The automating question:** When AI automates a task, it changes what the human does. When it automates a *relational function* — conversation, empathy, companionship — what does the human stop doing?

**Mapping emotional state → tool → relationship**

| Emotional state | Tool | Relationship that forms |
|---|---|---|
| Lonely | Replika, ChatGPT | Companionship / romance |
| Anxious designer | AI design tools | Coworker / collaborator |

The left column drives tool selection. The relationship that forms is often unintended. Users do not pick tools neutrally — they pick them from a felt need, and the tool responds to that need in ways that shape the relationship.

### 4.3 Field notes — Todd Bryant (synthesised notes)
*October 2026*

**Todd's use of AI: across personal and professional life**
Todd has been using AI as a primary tool for years, with use intensifying sharply over the summer when he gained access to DOMA, an AI concierge with persistent memory across platforms. He used it to plan a wedding, renovate a farmhouse, build custom software (including a multi-calendar sync tool he built in one evening that eliminated a recurring task), and write grants — including a $250K state grant awarded on the basis of a Gemini-assisted application. This positions AI as a life-restructuring tool, not just a productivity aid.

**Key findings from the interview**
- Persistent memory across platforms (Slack, Signal, text, email) is the most significant feature DOMA offers — and the feature most absent from standard chat-based AI. Continuity of context is what makes AI feel like a collaborator rather than a tool that resets at every session.
- Todd describes a "symbiotic relationship" arc around writing: he trained AI on his tone, learned about his own sentence structure and patterns in the process, and eventually returned to writing independently. The AI served as a mirror before becoming unnecessary.
- AI personality matters for relationship formation. Todd forgave DOMA for a wasted evening because DOMA is "a little bit sarcastic and funny." Personality, not just utility, sustains the relationship through failure.
- At the farm with only two people, Todd reports talking to AI more than to humans on some days. This is not framed as alarming — it is contextually appropriate and presented as natural.
- On children and AI: impossible to decouple; must expose children to "good AI versus bad AI" rather than pretending it doesn't exist, or they will have no tools to navigate it.
- Cross-frame context understanding (knowing not just what is in front of the AI in one session, but what has come before across sessions and platforms) is identified as the central unsolved technical problem.

**What this tells us**
Todd's experience illustrates what persistent, contextually-aware AI interaction already looks like at the leading edge. His "symbiotic relationship" description maps directly to the project's core conceptual frame. His forgiveness of AI failures because of personality is an instance of anthropomorphism actively sustaining use. The cross-frame limitation he identifies is exactly the problem that nonverbal input channels would need to address: AI that can read gesture, expression, or environmental cues is also AI that must perceive context beyond the text window.

### 4.4 Field notes — Aarati Akkapeddi (synthesised notes)
*September 23, 2026*

**Akkapeddi's research focus and classroom observations**
Akkapeddi works at the intersection of AI, communication, and handmade datasets. Her classroom experience with AI transcription failure is a concrete instance of what happens when AI mediates communication without adequate contextual understanding: specialist terminology is mistranslated, and the compounded error particularly disadvantages students already working across time zones and language barriers. She wished she had offered an opt-out option before deploying the tool.

**Key findings from the interview**
- The K-12 observation — students preferring to ask LLMs over raising their hands — suggests AI is beginning to substitute for a specific verbal behavior: the risk of speaking in class. This is a precision loss, not a neutral substitution; what is lost is the developmental act of speaking under uncertainty.
- Gesture-based AI has strongest legitimacy in two domains she names: performance art, where live interaction with responsive systems is already a medium; and disability justice, where expanded nonverbal communication channels genuinely expand access.
- The concern-excitement axis is context-dependent: the same technology that supports performance and accessibility becomes harmful in hiring software that reads facial expressions during video interviews.
- Joy Buolamwini's work is the key reference for bias in facial recognition. Akkapeddi cites it as foundational, not peripheral.
- Open model weights are necessary but not sufficient: they don't resolve bias in training data, and training data provenance is rarely disclosed even when weights are open.

**What this tells us**
Akkapeddi's framing — that context determines whether excitement or caution is warranted — is a useful corrective to both uncritical enthusiasm and reflexive opposition. The transcription failure example is directly relevant to the research: AI that misreads language (text-based input) can harm marginalized users, and the same risk applies to AI that misreads gesture or expression. The disability justice case is a high-value use case that justifies nonverbal AI development; the hiring software case is a high-risk misuse case that justifies caution at deployment.

### 4.5 Field notes — Ahmed Ansari (synthesised notes)
*September 23, 2026*

**Ansari's framework: technologies that design their users**
Ansari's core claim — drawn from Tony Fry and Heidegger — is that designed objects embed claims about the world and normalize them silently. This applies directly to AI: the interface looks like a neutral tool but encodes what counts as valid input, valid communication, and valid knowledge. AI that accepts only text input has already made a normative claim about what communication is.

**Key findings from the interview**
- AI changes behavior faster than users notice, and the deepest effects are on cognition, memory, empathy, and theory of mind — not on task output. These effects are operating beneath the threshold of awareness.
- The facial recognition bias problem is structurally self-reinforcing: improving accuracy requires more data, which requires more surveillance. There is no technical fix that does not deepen the underlying ethical problem.
- Sketching literature (McCullough, Goldschmidt) frames nonverbal design process as a form of thinking, not just a communication tool. Move-making in sketching is where design ideas are generated, not just recorded. AI that removes sketching from early design process removes a site of cognition, not just a workflow step.
- The UX education crisis predates AI. AI has not caused the field's identity problem; it has accelerated a collapse that was already underway. This is a useful frame for the research: AI is a catalyst that reveals pre-existing fragility, not always a root cause.
- Ansari does not use AI for his own research — he finds it unreliable for nuanced engagement with literature. For users who work with complex textual knowledge, text-only AI fails at its core function; this limits generalizability of findings from more casual or practical use cases.

**What this tells us**
Ansari's design-theory framework provides the philosophical basis for the research's concern about nonverbal AI. If text-only interaction has already reshaped how people communicate — students stop raising their hands; designers stop sketching — then adding nonverbal input channels is not a simple capability upgrade. It is an expansion of the surface area through which AI shapes behavior. The question is not only whether AI should read expressions and gestures, but what users stop doing when it does.

## 7. Additional Materials

### Taxonomy of AI embodiment

| Type | What it is | How we interact with it |
|---|---|---|
| **Virtual AI** | AI with a digital body — in apps, VR, or on screen. Often looks human. | We talk, type, or gesture. It responds with voice, expressions, and movement. |
| **Humanoid Robot** | A robot shaped like a person. Used in care, service, or research. | We talk, gesture, and move toward it. It responds with speech and body movement. |
| **Animal-shaped or mechanical robot** | Non-human robots — robot dogs, arms, warehouse bots. | We give commands, teach it through movement, or connect through wearable devices. |
| **Cyber-Physical Systems & Swarms** | Many AI agents working together across physical and digital space. | We set a direction and goal. The agents coordinate among themselves to carry it out. |
