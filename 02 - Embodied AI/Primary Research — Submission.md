# Primary Research Documentation
**Nonverbal Human-AI Interaction — Pre-Thesis Project**
Issac Ting, Eros, Sid · October 2026

---

## 1. Research Overview

This project explores how people interact with AI today, and what might change if AI could understand more than just text. Almost all human-AI interaction currently happens through typing or speech that gets converted to text. But people communicate in many other ways — through gesture, expression, sketching, tone of voice, and physical presence. This research examines what gets lost in text-only interaction, and what opening up nonverbal channels might mean for how people relate to AI.

**Research Questions**

- **RQ1:** How are people using AI today, and what forms does it take in their lives?
- **RQ2:** How does giving AI a physical or virtual body change the way people relate to it?
- **RQ3:** Does the type of body AI has affect how much people trust it, feel connected to it, or follow its guidance?

## 2. Interview Guide and Protocol

### Research briefing (read to all participants at the start of each interview)

Thank you for taking the time to speak with us. Before we begin, we'd like to give you some context on what we're working on.

The three of us are working together on our pre-thesis research, and our topic is nonverbal human-AI interaction. Today, almost all of our communication with AI happens through text or speech. Even when we speak to it through dictation tools, our words are still turned into text before the AI responds. But that's not how people naturally communicate with each other. When we explain an idea, we point, sketch, gesture, show objects, and use facial expressions. A lot of meaning lives outside of words.

We're interested in what it would mean for AI to understand us in those ways too — through drawing, scribbling, gesturing, showing things, and expressing ourselves, without having to translate every thought into text first.

We're at an early, exploratory stage. The three of us will research this space together first, and then each of us will carve out our own direction for our individual theses. That's why we're speaking with experts like you. We're not looking for feedback on a specific study. We'd like to hear how you think about this subject, drawing on your own experience and expertise, so we can understand it more deeply and find directions we may not have considered.

There are no right or wrong answers. Please feel free to take any question wherever you find it most interesting, share stories or examples, or tell us if you think we're framing something the wrong way.

Would it be all right if we recorded this conversation or took notes, just for our own research purposes?

---

### Core 5 questions

1. Can you tell us a bit about your work or practice?
2. What have you noticed about how AI shapes the way people behave or communicate?
3. Tell me about a time AI completely misunderstood you. What got lost?
4. If AI could understand your nonverbal communication — gestures, sketches, expressions — what would you use it for?
5. How would you feel if an AI could read your facial expressions, emotions, and tone of voice? What excites or concerns you about that?

---

### Follow-up prompts

1. So much of how we communicate is nonverbal — pointing, sketching on a napkin, a raised eyebrow. Why do you think our interaction with AI has been reduced almost entirely to text?
2. When you explain an idea to a colleague or student, how much of it happens through gesture, drawing, or showing? Can you recall a time when words alone weren't enough?
3. What gets lost when we have to translate a thought into text before an AI can understand it?
4. If you could point, scribble, or gesture at an AI and it truly understood you, what's the first thing you would use it for?
5. What would it mean for an AI to "understand" a gesture or a sketch? Is that the same as understanding a sentence?
6. Should nonverbal input replace text and speech, or work alongside them? What might that combination look like?
7. Should the AI respond nonverbally too — by sketching, showing, or reacting? Or does that feel like a step too far?
8. Right now AI lives inside a screen. Nonverbal interaction would require AI to "see" us. How might it feel to be watched by the thing you're talking to?
9. Many gestures and expressions are culturally specific. Whose nonverbal language would AI end up learning by default, and who might be left out?
10. Are there historical or non-Western ideas about communication — craft traditions, ritual, tacit knowledge — that could change how we design AI for nonverbal interaction?

### Interview 1 — Kevin Chow
**Date:** September 25, 2026
**Participant:** Kevin Chow, PhD researcher in human-computer interaction
**How contacted:** Personal connection through Issac (a mutual colleague, Sid, made the introduction)
**Format:** Remote video call. Full transcript in accompanying PDF: *Meeting Title: Issac <> Kevin.pdf*

**Context:** Kevin's PhD research included a study in which a computer vision system tracked nonverbal cues — smiling frequency, nodding rate, posture — in real work video meetings. The study was inspired by Zoom's move toward emotion AI — software that classifies users' emotional states from facial expressions and other nonverbal cues — during the pandemic.

**Key excerpts from transcript:**

> "We explored more specifically looking at cues instead of emotions. At a much lower level, looking at things like smiling or nodding — because those are more objective things. I can actually capture and see how many times someone is smiling without necessarily saying something about what that means."

> "People found that there were a lot of times where they didn't care to have ideal or good nonverbal cues. Imperfect nonverbal cues — maybe I'm not smiling a lot, or I look really tired — especially in meetings where you were close with your colleagues, those were actually seen as being really important. They felt more authentic. The imperfection is what makes it human."

> "In the design of the system, we were very careful to keep it neutral. One thing we could have done is say 'you should smile more.' Instead, we said 'you haven't smiled in the last two minutes.' These are your metrics. Do what you want with them."

> "I would say scope it tightly to a specific context and specific goal. In general, you wouldn't be able to say much — I would say a lot of the time I probably don't need it. In some contexts, it could be very, very useful, but it's still difficult to decide how to act on it."

> "Paralanguage is the non-word aspects of your voice that communicate meaning — your tone, emotional quality, pitch, speaking rate, pauses and silences. It would be very interesting. I don't know if there's too much research on that. I'm not sure whether voice AI uses this or just does speech to text and only looks at the text."

### Interview 4 — Ahmed Ansari (Office Hours)
**Date:** September 23, 2026
**Participant:** Ahmed Ansari (aa7703@nyu.edu), PhD in design studies; faculty
**How contacted:** NYU faculty office hours, attended by Sid
**Format:** Office hours conversation. Notes in accompanying file: *Interview with Ahmed Ansari.md*

**Context:** Ansari's PhD research focuses on the intellectual and conceptual history of design — how ideas about research and design shift over time. He does not use AI for his own research but uses AI as a creative tool in studio and game design courses.

**Notes from interview:**

- Core framework: "the things we design, design us" (Tony Fry [citation needed], drawing on Heidegger) — technologies embed normative claims about how the world is and how it should be
- AI changes practices, establishes new norms, and habituates users silently and faster than noticed; deeper effects on cognition, memory, empathy, and theory of mind
- Affective computing and facial recognition: accuracy drops for darker skin tones and female-presenting faces. Misidentification is used by police and government agencies, disproportionately harming people of color and women. Improving accuracy requires more surveillance data, which creates its own ethical problem.
- Sketching and drawing as nonverbal creative process: literature from Malcolm McCullough (*Abstracting Craft*) and Gabriella Goldschmidt on move-making and reflective practice; CAD tools changed but did not eliminate craft; AI poses a similar but faster disruption
- Students are sketching less and moving to tools too early; this undermines design thinking development
- UX as a profession is only about twenty years old, with no established benchmarks or standards. AI has accelerated role collapse — designers are now expected to do research, development, strategy, and management — but the field's identity crisis predates AI by twenty-five years. "The field is an absolute disaster and we're in a world of trouble."

## 4. Field Notes

### 4.1 Kevin Chow
*September 25, 2026*

**Kevin's research: nonverbal cue tracking in video meetings**
Kevin's team used computer vision to monitor body language and facial expressions in real work video calls. The project was inspired by Zoom's push to add emotion AI during the pandemic. The team made a deliberate methodological decision to track observable cues — smiling frequency, nodding rate, posture — rather than attempting to classify emotional states. The reasoning: a cue is a measurable observable; an emotion is an inference layered on top of a cue, one that varies across individuals and cultures. The system was designed to report neutrally ("you haven't smiled in a minute") rather than to prescribe ("you should smile more").

**Key findings from that research**
- After several weeks of use, participants often did not want to optimize their nonverbal behavior. In close, familiar meetings, appearing tired or visibly struggling was valued as authentic — a signal that could prompt a colleague to check in. Kevin described this as: *the imperfection is what makes it human.*
- In formal settings — presentations, job interviews, teaching — participants found nonverbal feedback genuinely useful. They needed support for managing how they were coming across.
- The formal/informal axis is consistent with technology-mediated communication research broadly: AI-mediated tools are more acceptable and more useful in high-stakes, formal contexts.
- People overcompensate on Zoom because they see their own image on screen — a form of performative self-monitoring that drives Zoom fatigue.

**On gesture and expression input for AI**
- Nonverbal input is not universally useful — whether it helps depends entirely on the task and context. For simple text editing, gesture reading is unnecessary and risks misinterpretation.
- Computer vision struggles with ambiguity: concentration looks like frustration from the outside.
- Culture plays a significant role. AI trained on internet data risks encoding stereotyped mappings — equating Western users with expressiveness, Eastern users with restraint — as defaults.
- Paralanguage (tone, pitch, speaking rate, pauses) is an underexplored channel. It is unclear whether voice AI currently captures it or discards it during transcription.
- Potential high-value contexts: customer service frustration detection, therapy.

### 4.2 Seminar field notes
*October 2026*

Our research interest within the group converged towards a shared interest in how we interact with AI, and also exploring the possible ways we can expand beyond current modes of interactions. Where my interests diverge between the other two team members was I was less focused on the actual interaction and more interested in the social dynamics that shift as a result of those interactions. So my secondary research as well as qualitative coding (systematic labelling of themes across transcripts) deviates from the interaction and modality layer and looks a bit more at the underlying cognitive behaviour and sociological factors.

**How does conversation shape the way we understand the world?**
- People will find ways to connect — the drive toward connection is fundamental
- Observing → generating insight → people are lonely → we will go to great lengths to avoid loneliness

**On digital adoption**
- Not all users adapt to new technology at the same rate
- Multiple chat platforms: what does it mean for how people relate to AI when they have different relationships with different systems?

**Two axes that emerged from discussion**

```
Instrumental ←————————————→ Relational
                    ↕
         Prescribed behavior
                    ↕
         Emergent behavior
```

- **Instrumental** — using AI as a tool to get something done
- **Relational** — relating to AI as an entity
- **Prescribed** — the interface tells you how to behave
- **Emergent** — behavior arises unpredictably from the interaction

**The automating question:** When AI automates a task, it changes what the human does. When it automates a relational function — conversation, empathy, companionship — what does the human stop doing?

**Mapping emotional state → tool → relationship**

| Emotional state | Tool | Relationship that forms |
|---|---|---|
| Lonely | Replika, ChatGPT | Companionship / romance |
| Anxious designer | AI design tools | Coworker / collaborator |

The left column drives tool selection. The relationship that forms is often unintended. Users do not pick tools neutrally — they pick them from a felt need, and the tool responds to that need in ways that shape the relationship.
