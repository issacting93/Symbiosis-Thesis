# Podcast script: Where did all the translators go?

Oct 13, 2026 · @Zac

## 1. Intro

Hi. My name is Issac. I'm a UX designer, and I've been doing UX design for about seven years. Then I came to New York to study. I'm currently doing my master's in engineering at NYU, and I'm also doing research on the side with one of my professors.

My personal interest is in history. I really enjoy seeing how things became the way they are. And I'm interested in the social side of technology. How does using technology affect our relationships with other people? And how does it affect our relationship with ourselves?

So that's a bit of context on where my interests lie. Design, history, and how tech shapes our relationships. Those three things are what this whole episode is about.

## 2. A bit of history

So let's start with a bit of history. Around the 14th century, Europe had just lived through one of the worst tragedies in its history. We now call it the Black Death. It killed somewhere between a third and a half of Europe. And one of the things Europe really needed to do was revitalise itself.

And we know, from a historical perspective, what came after: the Renaissance. A period we think of as one of wealth, of scientific discovery, of innovation, of art, of development in medicine.

So how did that come to be?

Well, there was a movement in Europe to go back to the classics. A sense of: hey, we've lost a lot of these old ideas, and values, and technology, and language. We need to go back to where we came from.

And here's the part we tend to forget. A lot of that knowledge hadn't actually disappeared. It had survived somewhere else. Monks had kept texts in monasteries. Islamic scholars had gathered Greek and Roman ideas, translated them into Arabic, added to them, and saved them in their archives. So what European scholars did was go out across the continent, and beyond it, to rediscover that knowledge. Because Europe had been fragmented by the fall of Rome, by constant war, and by the plague.

And what came out of it was a revival of Western culture. People reclaimed the ideas of Plato, of Aristotle, and so on, and brought back what was once lost.

And from a historical perspective, it worked, right? Artists started painting realistic portraits. You get people like Leonardo da Vinci, Raphael, Michelangelo. And a generation before them, Donatello, laying a lot of the groundwork they built on. All your Ninja Turtles. All from that same revival.

## 3. It took a whole network

Now, Europe really focused on rebuilding this culture. But I think one of the most important things is to look at the network of people that made it happen. Right?

You had the people who stored knowledge. And for a long time they were very isolated from the rest of the world. Monks in monasteries.

You had the Islamic scholars, who literally didn't speak the same language. So you needed people, often Europeans who grew up under Muslim rule, to help translate that knowledge. The Greek went into Arabic, and then the Arabic went into Latin, in places like Toledo in Spain. So you had the role of translators.

Then you had the educators. The people who taught this knowledge in schools. Someone like Chrysoloras, who came from Constantinople to teach Greek in Florence.

You also had critics. People who argued and debated, and checked whether things were actually true. And you had curators. People who chose what was worth keeping, and what was good quality.

These are the different roles that let a system of knowledge exist. Making knowledge. Reviving it. Storing it. And applying it.

## 4. UX is older than it feels

So when we think about knowledge, this is the kind of system that emerged to make it, store it, and pass it on. Keepers, translators, educators, critics, curators.

Now think about UX today. It feels like it only has a history of ten, maybe twenty years. After all, most people didn't have computers twenty years ago.

And the thing is, that's not really true.

The field of UX emerged because of translators. People like Don Norman. They took things that were quite obscure, buried in engineering research, in sociology, in psychology, and translated them into language that many people could understand.

And that let us educate people. It let us recontextualise things we might already have known, but didn't think were important. And then apply them to this new form of technology we now call the user interface. The thing that lets us interact with software.

But here's the thing. A lot of those studies weren't even done with software at the start. The famous example Norman uses is a door. Push or pull? That's a "Norman door." We took what we'd tested in the physical world, things like doors and ketchup bottles, and we applied it to websites and apps. Same underlying principles. Same methods we used to study physical products.

And I think that's really important to think about. Because we're now on the edge of a new era, so to speak. So people keep telling us, right? This is the age of AI. And it's started to change a lot of the interactions we have with technology. It's started to shape the way we use technology. And it's started to change the way we interact with each other.

## 5. We kept the methods, not the rigour

So here's where it gets interesting, right? As UX grew up and became an industry, we took a lot of the methods. Interviews, usability tests, personas, journey maps. All of that came out of research. But somewhere along the way we kept the methods and kind of dropped the rigour behind them.

And you can see it in the products. How many "Airbnb for X" apps have we seen? How many "Uber for X"? And now, honestly, how many products are basically ChatGPT with a different UI on top? It's a copy of a copy of a copy. Every time you copy something, you lose a bit of the reason it was designed that way in the first place.

And so the question I keep coming back to is, do we actually have the skills to know what the problems are? Not to build the thing. To know what's worth building.

Because here's the paradox. It has never been easier to build. You can go from an idea to a working prototype in an afternoon now. And yet, it feels like we're further than ever from genuinely new things. The bottleneck moved. It's not making anymore. It's understanding.

## 6. Her, and the Black Mirror effect

I want to talk about a film for a second. *Her*, by Spike Jonze, came out in 2013. Thirteen years ago. And if you watch it today, it doesn't really feel like science fiction anymore. It feels more like a documentary.

And I don't think that's because Spike Jonze predicted the future. I think it's because he observed the present. He looked at how lonely people were, how much we were already talking to our devices, and he translated that into a story. That's observation, not prediction. That's actually what a good researcher does, right?

Now here's the part that worries me. A lot of products today look like they were built straight out of dystopian sci-fi. The film was the warning, and somebody read it as a spec. I call it the Black Mirror effect. We watch the cautionary tale, and then we go and build the thing anyway.

And part of the reason, honestly, is marketing. If everyone already knows the story, it's a lot cheaper to sell. You don't have to explain the product. The movie already did that for you.

## 7. It comes down to trust

So a lot of this comes down to trust. And right now, a lot of the ways we're building trust are, let's say, cosmetic. Cute avatars. Little pet-like characters. A warm voice. Things that make AI *feel* trustworthy.

And there's a story that sums this up really well. In September 2023, Sam Altman reached out to Scarlett Johansson, the voice of Samantha in *Her*, and asked if she'd voice ChatGPT. The pitch, as she described it, was that her voice could help people feel comfortable with AI. She said no. Then in May 2024, OpenAI shipped a voice called "Sky", and a lot of people listening heard Samantha. Johansson said she was shocked. OpenAI said it wasn't her voice, but they paused it.

And I think that's the whole thing in one story, right? The goal was comfort. Feeling trustworthy.

But feeling trustworthy and being trustworthy are two different things. The easy fixes are the avatar, the voice, the tone. The hard fixes are explainability, transparency, alignment. Actually being able to say why the system did what it did, and making sure it does what we meant.

And the knowledge we need for the hard fixes exists. The problem is where it lives. A lot of it is locked away in academic papers nobody in industry reads. And a lot of it is locked away in proprietary data that never leaves the company. So the network today looks something like this. Lots of nodes. Not a lot of lines between them.

## 8. How you build a model, and what AI can't do

Let me zoom out for a second and talk about how you actually build a model of the world. Any model. A design, a product, a mental model.

It starts with observation. You watch people, you notice things. Those observations become signals. The signals get abstracted into something you can work with. And then that abstraction gets processed into a decision, a design, a product.

Now what AI has done, and it's genuinely impressive, is close the gap at the end of that chain. Design to code is basically solved, or close to it. AI is a really good translator from one abstraction to another.

But it can't observe. It wasn't in the room. It approximates the signals from what's already been written down, and there's always some error in that. It's working from proxy signals, signals of signals.

So it's still humans who have to make sense of the signals. Who have to go out and look. And when we skip that step, when we build off the proxies, we lose a lot of the reason behind things. Which, again, is the copy of a copy problem.

And that's really the core of this episode. We don't have a building problem. We have a translator problem. There just aren't enough people whose job is to take what we observe and turn it into something we can build on.

## 9. What we actually need

So what do we need? I think it's two ends of the same pipe.

On one end, we need translators. People like Don Norman, who could take research about how people think and turn it into something a designer could actually use. We had those people. We need a lot more of them now.

On the other end, we need downstream validation. Actually checking what happens after we ship. Understanding the consequences, not just the conversion rate. And for that we need better tools.

And right now, most of that work is happening in academia. Which is great, but it means it's not reaching the people building products. One area I'm really excited about is HCI evaluation for AI. Not measuring models by tokens and parameters, but measuring them against human metrics. Did it actually help? Did the person understand it? Did they come away better off?

There's a team at MIT working on this, a friend of mine, Rachel, is on it. It's called ImpactBench, and the idea is to benchmark AI on its impact on people, not just its performance on tasks. That's exactly the kind of bridge I'm talking about.

## 10. Close *(draft, not yet recorded)*

So if you're a designer or a researcher listening to this, here's what I'd take away. Three moves.

One, reconnect. Go read the research. Talk to the academics. Find the lines between the nodes, because that's where the value is.

Two, redefine. Your job isn't to make the screen. AI can make the screen. Your job is to be the translator. To observe, to make sense of it, and to check what happens after.

And three, lean into craft. The observation, the judgment, the rigour. That's the part that can't be automated, and that's the part we let slip.

I'll leave you with Poggio. Six hundred years ago, he walked into a monastery library and found a book nobody had read in a thousand years. He didn't write it. He just knew it mattered, and he made sure it got copied, translated, and passed on. That's the job. The knowledge is out there. Somebody has to go find it.

This has been *The Point of Interface*. Thanks for listening.
