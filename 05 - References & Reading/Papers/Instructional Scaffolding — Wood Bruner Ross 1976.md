# Instructional Scaffolding — Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976

**Key theorists:** Jerome Bruner, Lev Vygotsky (foundational), Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976)  
**Origin:** Bruner's children's language acquisition studies, late 1950s; formalised by Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976  
**Built on:** Vygotsky's Zone of Proximal Development

---

## Core Definition

Scaffolding is temporary support given to a learner that is calibrated to their current level of competence and gradually withdrawn as that competence develops. Three features define it:

1. **Collaborative interaction** — learner and a more knowledgeable other work together
2. **ZPD positioning** — the task is slightly beyond what the learner can do independently, but achievable with support
3. **Gradual withdrawal (fading)** — as the learner develops autonomous capability, the support is removed

The goal of scaffolding is always its own disappearance. A scaffold that never fades is not a scaffold — it is a substitute.

---

## The Scaffold/Substitute Distinction

This is the distinction that matters most for the Symbiosis framework.

**Scaffolding:** The support is calibrated to what the learner cannot yet do. It helps them do it, and in doing it they develop the capacity to do it themselves. Over time, the support becomes unnecessary and is withdrawn. The learner is more capable after the relationship than before.

**Substitution:** The support does something for the learner that they could have developed the capacity to do themselves. The support is never withdrawn because the learner never develops the underlying capacity. The learner is not more capable — they are more dependent.

Both look identical in the short term. In both cases, the learner gets the outcome they wanted. The difference only becomes visible over time: does the person's independent capacity grow or atrophy?

## Key Concepts

### Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD)
→ See [[Sociocultural Theory — Vygotsky 1934]]

The gap between what a learner can do independently and what they can do with support. Effective scaffolding targets this gap — not too easy (no growth), not too hard (overwhelm). The ZPD is dynamic; it shifts as competence increases.

### Fading
The process of gradually withdrawing support as the learner's competence develops. Fading is not abandonment — it is responsive reduction, matched to the learner's growing autonomy. A scaffold that does not fade has failed at its core purpose.

### Contingency
Effective scaffolding is contingent on the learner's current state — it adjusts in real time to what the learner needs. When the learner struggles, support increases. When the learner succeeds, support decreases. Non-contingent support (fixed, regardless of learner state) is less effective and more likely to drift toward substitution.

### Types of Scaffolding

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| Soft / contingent | Real-time, responsive to learner needs |
| Hard / embedded | Pre-planned supports for anticipated difficulties |
| Reciprocal | Peer-to-peer; shared scaffolding that evolves |
| Technical | Computer-based guidance (tutorials, prompts, examples) |

## Critiques

- **Cognitive Load Theory** argues that minimal guidance misaligns with how novice learners process information — beginners may need more direct instruction than scaffolding provides
- **Empirical gaps** — limited evidence for learner-centred versus teacher-led effectiveness in practice
- **Fading is hard** — in practice, supports often persist longer than they should, slipping from scaffold to crutch without a clear threshold

## Relevance to Symbiosis Research

Scaffolding is the positive target for the framework — what mutualistic AI looks like at the interaction level.

**The unit of analysis question:** Each time a person uses AI to accomplish something, the interaction is either scaffolding or substitution. The difference:
- **Scaffolding:** The AI helps the person do something they are developing the capacity to do themselves. The support is contingent on their current level. Over time, the person needs the AI less for this kind of task.
- **Substitution:** The AI does something the person could have learned to do. The person never develops the underlying capacity. Over time, they need the AI more.

This gives the framework its smallest unit. A parasitic relationship is not one dramatic event — it is an accumulation of substitution interactions that, individually, feel helpful and, collectively, erode the person's independent capacity.

**The two-track question:** Scaffolding at the task level does not automatically mean scaffolding at the social level. An AI can scaffold a writing task (the person becomes a better writer) while substituting for the social interaction that would have produced that feedback (the person becomes less likely to seek feedback from peers). The social capacity atrophies even as the task capacity grows.

This is where the technology impact and the human-relationship impact come apart — and where the most interesting research questions live.
