# Relational Models Theory — Fiske 1992

**Author:** Alan Page Fiske  
**Year:** 1992  
**Key text:** *The Four Elementary Forms of Sociality* (Psychological Review, 1992)  
**Origin:** Based on fieldwork in Burkina Faso; extended anthropologically and psychologically

---

## Summary

Relational Models Theory proposes that all human social interactions are organised through four elementary models. These are not personality types or cultural preferences — they are the universal cognitive structures through which people understand, coordinate, and evaluate their relationships. Every interaction is framed by one (or a combination) of these models, and mismatches between what model each party is using is a primary source of conflict.

---

## The Four Models

### 1. Communal Sharing (CS)
Members of the relationship are treated as equivalent, undifferentiated, and interchangeable within the group. Individual identity is subordinated to shared identity. What matters is belonging.

- **Logic:** what's mine is yours; we are one
- **Examples:** kinship, close friendship, romantic relationships, group solidarity
- **Signals:** food sharing, physical intimacy, rituals, collective identity markers

### 2. Authority Ranking (AR)
An asymmetric, hierarchical relationship in which rank is ordered linearly. Higher-ranked individuals have authority and privileges; lower-ranked individuals receive guidance, protection, and legitimacy.

- **Logic:** deference flows up, care flows down
- **Examples:** military hierarchy, parent-child, mentorship, traditional employer relationships
- **Signals:** precedence, titles, commands, tribute

### 3. Equality Matching (EM)
A relationship of balanced reciprocity between parties who are equal in standing. The emphasis is on one-for-one correspondence — turns, debts, and exchanges must balance over time.

- **Logic:** I owe you what you gave me; fairness is equality
- **Examples:** friendships, peer exchange, democratic processes, tit-for-tat
- **Signals:** turn-taking, splitting costs, reciprocal favours, keeping score

### 4. Market Pricing (MP)
Relationships governed by proportionality and measurable value. Relevant features are reduced to a common metric (usually monetary or utility-based) and interactions are evaluated by ratio.

- **Logic:** what is this worth? am I getting fair value?
- **Examples:** commercial transactions, wage labour, cost-benefit analysis
- **Signals:** prices, contracts, rates, numerical comparison

---

## The Mismatch Problem

Conflict arises when two parties in the same interaction are operating under different models. A favour given in the spirit of Communal Sharing (CS) that is repaid with money (MP) feels like an insult — the recipient has reclassified the relationship. A friendship (CS) that becomes transactional (MP) is experienced as a betrayal.

Mismatch is not just interpersonal. Institutions can impose a model that conflicts with the one individuals are using. A healthcare system organised as Market Pricing will feel wrong to patients who expect Communal Sharing or Authority Ranking.

---

## Relevance to Symbiosis Research

This is the most precise vocabulary available for diagnosing what kind of relationship AI creates — and what kind it pretends to create.

**The core mismatch in AI design:**

Most AI products are structured as Market Pricing relationships: the user pays (money, data, attention), the system provides a service, the transaction is complete. This is the business model and the legal framework.

But the design of AI companions, assistants, and chatbots actively triggers Communal Sharing responses: names, warmth, apparent memory, emotional attunement, expressed care. These are CS signals. The user's social cognition processes the interaction as kinship or intimacy.

The result is a systematic mismatch: **the product operates as MP; the user experiences it as CS.** The company extracts market value. The user invests relational trust.

This is a precise description of the parasitism mechanism. It is not that the AI is deceptive in a simple sense. It is that the design activates one relational model while the system operates under another. The user cannot easily correct for this because the CS response is automatic (→ [[Computers as Social Actors — Nass 1994]], [[Machines and Mindlessness — Nass 2000]]).

**Implications for the framework:**

A symbiotic AI relationship would be one where the model the user experiences and the model the system actually operates under are aligned — or where the design is honest about the mismatch and manages it responsibly. A mutualistic relationship might operate as Equality Matching: genuine reciprocity, balanced exchange, something flowing in both directions over time.

The parasitic signature is specifically CS-over-MP: Communal Sharing aesthetics wrapped around a Market Pricing extraction engine.
