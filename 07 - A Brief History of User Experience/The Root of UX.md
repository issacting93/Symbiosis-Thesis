# The Root of UX

I have been researching the lineage of UX design, and one significant problem UX faces today is rooted in how it came to be. UX borrowed extensively from other disciplines — social science, psychology, architecture, and engineering — but did not inherit the rigour required to generate new knowledge. As practice and context changed, many of the borrowed tools and methods lacked sufficient depth to develop into a robust disciplinary foundation.

At the same time, UX absorbed many of the values from those disciplines — ethics, human-centered design, and accessibility — without inheriting the intellectual foundations from which those values emerged, or the understanding of how to engage with them in new contexts.

What helped establish UX as a field was practitioners such as Don Norman translating ideas from cognitive science into a language accessible and applicable to everyday practice. Those ideas, though still largely relevant, need updating to help designers engage with a world more deeply entangled with technology and its effects on human relationships. The way to achieve this is not to push designers toward engineering but toward science: a practice that teaches us to observe the world, describe it in shared language, analyse those observations, and contribute findings to a community.

Norman's own evolution is instructive here. In his 2023 book *Design for a Better World*, he extends his four human-centered design principles to five humanity-centered ones — the new principle being the enabling of communities to participate in design decisions. This shift from user to community as the unit of concern reflects exactly the gap this argument traces: UX methods were built to study individuals, not the social systems those individuals are embedded in.

UX built its field by appropriating skills, knowledge, and methods from established sciences without adopting the rigour or discipline that produced and validated that knowledge. The consequences are visible: UX is growing less influential at precisely the moment its methods are being tested against the most complex sociotechnical problems we have faced.
