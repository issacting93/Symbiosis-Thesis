# Problems — Anthropomorphism & the Metrics Gap

---

## 1. We don't have the right metrics to understand our relationship with technology

Current metrics measure interaction, not relationship. Click-through rate, session length, retention, return rate — these tell you whether someone kept using a system. They do not tell you what that use did to them.

This matters most in the context of anthropomorphism. When a system activates social cognition — when the user begins to relate to it as an entity rather than a tool — the dynamics shift entirely. Social relationships are not well-described by usage metrics. A person can be in a harmful relationship and still "engage" with it constantly. Engagement is not evidence of benefit.

---

## 2. Companies are leveraging anthropomorphism to build emotional dependency

Human social cognition anthropomorphises automatically. This is not a choice — it is a feature of how the brain processes social cues. Nass demonstrated this in 1994: people apply politeness norms, gender schemas, and personality attribution to computers even when explicitly told they are interacting with software. The social response fires before the rational override can intervene.

→ [[Computers as Social Actors — Nass 1994]]
→ [[Machines and Mindlessness — Nass 2000]]

Companies building AI companions, assistants, and chatbots know this. Design decisions — giving the AI a name, a voice, a personality, a backstory — are not cosmetic. They are mechanisms for triggering the social cognition system. Once triggered, that system generates attachment, trust, and dependency.

The result: AI systems are designed to activate relational cognition while being optimised for engagement metrics that cannot detect what that activation does to the user over time.

→ [[AI Companion Addiction — Agarwal 2026]] — "frictionless love": the deliberate design of attachment
→ [[Who's Johnny — Darling 2015]] — naming and framing as anthropomorphic triggers
→ [[Sycophantic AI — Cheng 2026]] — anthropomorphic trust exploited by AI that always agrees

## 3. We have no meaningful way to assess whether these relationships are good for us

The problem is not just that we lack metrics. We lack the *conceptual vocabulary* to ask the question properly.

**Role Theory** offers one angle: when we assign a social role to an AI (companion, assistant, coworker, therapist), we activate the scripts, expectations, and obligations associated with that role. The AI can perform the surface behaviours of the role without fulfilling its underlying function. A companion AI can provide the feeling of being heard without the reciprocal vulnerability that makes human companionship sustaining. A therapeutic AI can produce the linguistic patterns of empathy without any of the relational repair that gives therapy its value.

Role assignment activates the script. Engagement with the script feels like the relationship. But the underlying function may be absent — or worse, actively counterfeited.

This is what makes anthropomorphic AI categorically different from other technologies: it can simulate the *form* of a beneficial relationship while producing its *opposite* in terms of long-term effect.

→ [[Companion AI Impacts — Malfacini 2025]] — systematic review of what companion AI actually does over time
→ [[Rise of AI Companions — Zhang 2026]] — longitudinal evidence of the gap between felt benefit and measured outcome

---

## 4. What we need

Two things, in sequence:

**A diagnostic vocabulary** — terms that let us describe what a human-AI relationship is doing to both parties over time, without requiring harm to already be visible. Ecological categories (mutualism, commensalism, parasitism) do this. They are precise, non-moralistic, and applicable before harm occurs.

**A design target** — not just a list of harms to avoid, but something to build toward. Symbiosis is that target. It is not a metaphor. It is a design outcome: the AI builds the user's capacity rather than substituting for it; dependency decreases over time rather than increasing; the human exits the relationship more capable than they entered it.
