## 2. Specific outlets

| Source | What it contributes | Use in our paper | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Iacovides & Mekler (2019), "The role of gaming during difficult life experiences," CHI 2019 | Qualitative analysis of an online survey (95 participants): games gave respite, helped with feelings, supported social connection and growth, but were also called unproductive | Gaming as an outlet. **Also a precedent for a survey-based study at CHI** | ✅ |
| Zheng, Li, Gui & Luo (2025), "Customizing Emotional Support: How Do Individuals Construct and Interact With LLM-Powered Chatbots," CHI 2025 | Week-long field study with the ChatLab tool, then interviews and design activities, 22 participants; people enriched personas to foster open conversation | AI as an outlet; method precedent for week-long study plus follow-up. The list had three authors; Yuhan Luo is a fourth | ✅ (arXiv 2504.12943; the "research through design" label is not confirmed) |
| De Freitas, Uğuralp, Oğuz-Uğuralp & Puntoni (2026), "AI Companions Reduce Loneliness," *Journal of Consumer Research* | Talking to an AI companion lowers loneliness in the moment about as much as talking to a person, and more than watching YouTube; the mechanism is feeling heard | **Counterpoint to the hunch that AI is a minor outlet.** Note the limit: the relief is momentary, with no evidence of a lasting drop over a week | ✅ (from secondary summaries; arXiv 2407.19096; read the paper) |
| Mildner et al. (2023), "Defending Against the Dark Arts: Recognising Dark Patterns in Social Media," DIS 2023 | Types of dark patterns in four mobile social apps and how well people recognise them | DIS anchor for the "displacing" side. It is about recognition of manipulative design, not regulation, so use it for the design argument and not the empirical one | ✅ |

**Gap:** these papers each look at one outlet. None maps outlets against each other, or against other people, over time.

**Displacement debate**

| Source | What it contributes | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Kraut et al. (1998), "Internet Paradox"; follow-up (2002) | HomeNet: more internet use went with less family communication and more loneliness; the 2002 follow-up found effects largely gone, with sociable people gaining ("rich get richer") | ⚠️ |
| Valkenburg & Peter (c. 2007–2009), stimulation hypothesis | Online communication can strengthen existing friendships, especially among adolescents | ⚠️ |
| Hall et al. (2019), two tests of social displacement | Little evidence that social media use reduces face-to-face time | ⚠️ |

*Takeaway: "technology displaces people" is too simple; effects depend on who uses it and why.*

**Withdrawal experiments**

| Source | What it contributes | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Allcott et al. (2020), Facebook deactivation | Randomised experiment, four weeks off Facebook: more offline time with friends and family, small wellbeing gains | ⚠️ |
| Hunt et al. (2018) | Limiting social media for three weeks reduced loneliness and depression | ⚠️ |

*Both measure outcomes; neither asks what filled the gap or how relationships rearranged. Our current design does not include a withdrawal condition (see note below).*

**AI and relationships**

| Source | What it contributes | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Fang et al. (2025), "How AI and Human Behaviors Shape Psychosocial Effects of Extended Chatbot Use: A Longitudinal Randomized Controlled Study" (arXiv 2503.17473) | MIT Media Lab and OpenAI. 981 participants, 4 weeks, over 300,000 messages; three interaction modes by three conversation types. No significant effects of the conditions. Participants who chose to use the chatbot more did worse regardless of assignment; trust and social attraction went with more dependence and problematic use. The heavy-use finding is correlational | ✅ (abstract) |
| Guingrich & Graziano (2025), "A Longitudinal Randomized Control Study of Companion Chatbot Use: Anthropomorphism and Its Mediating Role on Social Impacts" (arXiv 2509.19515) | 183 participants, 21 days, companion chatbot versus word games, 10 minutes a day. No overall change in social outcomes; people who wanted more connection anthropomorphised more, and the effect on human relationships was mediated by anthropomorphism | ✅ (abstract) |
| Skjuve et al. (2021); Brandtzaeg et al. (2022) | Interviews with Replika users; social penetration theory | ⚠️ |
| Vallor (2015), moral deskilling | Delegating care and moral practice to machines can wear down the skills involved. Closest theoretical support for the substitution question; pair with Risko & Gilbert (2016) on cognitive offloading and ask whether emotional offloading behaves similarly | ⚠️ |

**What it means for positioning.** Across these strands, average effects are small and the action is in individual differences and motivation. Large studies show *that* this heterogeneity exists but not *what it consists of*. Candidate positioning sentence: *Large-scale studies find that technology's social effects depend on individual motivation and use; we examine how people themselves experience technology as scaffolding or substituting for their emotional skills and relationships.*

**Connections to the existing argument**
- De Freitas et al. (momentary relief, no lasting effect), Fang et al. (use level matters more than condition) and Guingrich & Graziano (anthropomorphism as mediator) agree that the *person* matters more than the *technology*.
- Vallor's moral deskilling is the philosophical form of the substitution side of the scaffolding test.

## Positioning

**Delta in one sentence.** Existing digital emotion regulation work catalogues what individuals do with technology; this work maps how technologies and relationships interrelate and shift over time, and uses the participant's own graph as a reflective design probe.

**Three claimed extensions of Smith et al. (2022):**
1. Time: longer than one week, or a structured follow-up.
2. Relations: people and technologies as nodes in one network.
3. Design contribution: a symbiotic versus parasitic framework that is usable by designers.

**What the check changed**
- De Freitas et al. is solid and recent, and it is the main challenge to the claim that AI barely matters. Its finding that relief is momentary supports a longitudinal design: single-session studies can't see whether outlets help or displace over time.
- Iacovides & Mekler shows that a survey study of a single outlet was accepted at CHI. That helps the survey-pilot route, as long as the survey is paired with something that gives it a design contribution.
- Hoefer & Voida is a DIS 2023 paper with nearly your interview method. Read it first for how they framed the contribution.
