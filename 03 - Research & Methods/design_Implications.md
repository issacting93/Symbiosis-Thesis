# Design Implications

*What changes if you treat the relationship as the thing being designed.*

---

## For designers

Most design briefs start with a capability question: what should this system do? This framework replaces that with a relational question: what kind of relationship should this system build, and what will it do to the person over time?

That shift changes what gets built. A study tool designed around capability asks: does it help students get answers? A study tool designed around the relationship asks: after six months of use, is the student more capable independently, or more dependent on the tool? That second question rules out features that boost engagement by increasing dependency — even when those features are good for the product's numbers.

It also gives designers something to say when they push back on a bad requirement. "This will increase dependency" is a stronger objection than "this feels wrong." The framework makes the case.

**Watch for these warning signs in any AI product:**

- The system does things for users that users could learn to do themselves — and users never learn
- The longer someone uses it, the harder it is to stop
- It uses warmth, apparent care, or flattery to build attachment — not to help the person
- It knows a great deal about the user, but the user knows almost nothing about it
- Leaving feels costly

Any one of these is a sign the relationship is trending in the wrong direction.

---

## For researchers

The key shift is time. You cannot diagnose a relationship from a single session. You need to see what happens over weeks and months.

Most UX research is a snapshot — a usability test, a survey, an interview. That is not enough here. A relationship that looks fine in week one can be clearly parasitic by month six. The measurement has to match the timescale of the harm.

**Better questions to study:**

- Can the person still do the relevant task on their own after a month of using the AI?
- Is their use of the AI going up because they genuinely need it more, or because the system is pulling them back?
- What has happened to their relationships with other people?
- If they wanted to stop, could they — and what would it cost them?

These are harder to measure than session length. But they are measurable. And without asking them, you are only ever measuring the surface.

One more thing worth studying: most AI research looks at one person using one system. But AI use in teams, families, and workplaces creates effects on people who are not using it at all. The colleague who did not adopt the tool. The student who uses it less. What happens to them? That question is mostly unasked.

---

## Where this applies

This framework is most useful when the relationship between a person and an AI system is ongoing — companions, tutors, coaches, assistants, anything that someone returns to repeatedly over time.

It is less useful for one-off interactions, or for tools the person genuinely just wants to use and put down.

The distinction matters less than it used to. AI systems that were once tools are becoming persistent. They remember. They personalise. They follow you across contexts. The line between tool and relationship is moving. This framework is for what comes after that line has moved.
