# Motivations

*Heilmeier Catechism — four questions every researcher should be able to answer.*

---

## What are you trying to do?

I am trying to study our relationship with technology using an ecological lens.

We spend more and more time with AI. These systems are designed to keep us coming back — more messages, more time, more dependence. That is not an accident. It is what they are built to do.

But we have no good way to tell whether these relationships are good for us. We can measure how much we use them. We cannot measure what they do to us over time.

I want to build a way of seeing these relationships clearly — using the same categories biologists use to describe relationships between living things. Some relationships help both sides. Some help one side and do nothing to the other. Some help one side and hurt the other. I think those categories fit what happens between people and AI. And I think designers need to know the difference before they build.

One clarification that matters: this is not about merging humans and technology. Most existing metaphors — the cyborg, the centaur — describe a fusion where the boundary between human and machine dissolves. That framing makes the relationship invisible. You cannot ask "is this good for the human?" if there is no longer a separate human to ask about.

This project keeps them separate. Human and technology are two distinct entities. The object of study is the relationship between them — what it does to each side over time, and whether that is beneficial or harmful.

---

## How is it done today, and what are the limits of current practice?

Today, companies measure how much people use their products. How often they come back. How long they stay. How many messages they send. These numbers are treated as signs of success.

The problem is that these numbers cannot tell you whether the person is better off. Someone can talk to an AI every day and become more lonely. They can use it for everything and slowly lose the ability to do things on their own. The numbers would look great. The person would be worse off. The measurement cannot see the harm because the harm is not what it is measuring.

There are people who critique this — people who study the ethics of technology. But they mostly tell you something is wrong after it has already happened. They do not give a designer something to use at the start.

There is no shared way in the design world to ask: what kind of relationship is this building over time?

---

## What is new in your approach and why do you think it will be successful?

The ecological way of thinking is not new. Biologists have used it for over a century. Anthropologists have used it to study how people live together. What is new is bringing it to the design of AI.

It gives two things that are missing today.

First, a way to name what is happening. Helpful, harmless, harmful. Plain words for what a relationship does to both sides over time.

Second, a direction to aim for. It is not just a critique. It is a target. You can design toward relationships that help both sides. That means: build people up instead of replacing them. Make them less dependent over time, not more. Make sure they come out of it more capable, not less.

I think it will work because the hard part is not the method. People already know how to study relationships over time. The hard part is asking the right question. This is the right question.

---

## Who cares?

Designers get a way to check their work that is not just "do people use it?" They get something to use at the start of a project, not just a list of harms to avoid at the end.

Companies putting AI in schools, care homes, and workplaces can ask a real question: is this making people more capable or less?

Policymakers who regulate AI companions and social AI get a way to tell the difference between acceptable and harmful — not just "is the AI lying?" but "does the relationship it creates serve the person?"

And people who use AI get words to describe what is happening to them. That matters. You cannot resist something you cannot name.

The bigger point: right now, AI is pulled toward relationships that take from people because taking is easier to measure than helping. A design approach built on helping both sides does not just criticize that. It gives people a way to build something else.
