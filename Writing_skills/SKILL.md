# Cormen's Rules of Usage

A checklist of 39 usage and punctuation rules, drawn from Thomas Cormen's
*Rules of Usage* (Dartmouth COSC 91/191), itself distilling Strunk & White's
*The Elements of Style* and Dupré's *Bugs in Writing*, plus Cormen's own
preferences. The rules target careful, formal English — academic and technical
writing above all.

## How to use this skill

**When drafting prose**, keep the rules in mind but don't let them freeze the
writing. Get the sentence down, then check it.

**When editing or proofreading**, make a deliberate pass. Most rules are
catch-on-sight; a handful reward an active search through the text:
- **which-hunting** — search every `which`; if the clause is restrictive
  (removing it changes the meaning), it should be `that` with no comma (Rule 30).
- **only** — search every `only` and confirm it sits immediately before the
  term it limits (Rule 36).
- **very** and **one of the most** — search and delete or sharpen (Rules 34, 26).
- **it's / its** and **affect / effect** — search; these are the errors most
  likely to survive a read-through (Rules 1, 16).
- **comprised of** — search; it's always wrong (Rule 14).

**When giving feedback**, cite the specific rule so the writer can internalize
it, e.g. "comma splice here (Rule 5) — use a semicolon or split the sentence."

**Read `references/cormen-rules.md`** for the full statement of any rule with
examples. The index below is a reminder, not the whole rule — consult the
reference before correcting anything subtle (Rules 3, 9, 13, 30, 38, 39 in
particular reward a full read).

## A note on the idiosyncratic rules

A few rules reflect Cormen's own taste more than settled consensus, and the
writer may reasonably override them: **Rule 25** (avoid singular *they* —
increasingly accepted, and the Chicago Manual has softened), **Rule 28**
(*shall* vs. *will* — largely archaic), and **Rule 32** (never *aforementioned*).
Apply them by default for a formal academic register, but flag rather than force
them, and defer to a house style guide (ACM, APA, Chicago) where one governs.

## The 39 rules — quick index

### Punctuation
1. **Possessive** — form the singular possessive with `'s` even after *s*
   (*Farkas's Lemma*); plural possessive takes a bare apostrophe
   (*the computers' monitors*). *it's* = *it is*; *its* = belonging to it.
2. **Serial comma** — always use it in lists of three or more. Use semicolons
   between list items that themselves contain commas.
3. **Parenthetical expressions** — set off with commas (least parenthetical),
   or parentheses / em-dashes (more). Rule of thumb: if you'd lower your voice,
   use parentheses; if not, dashes. Nonrestrictive → commas; restrictive → none.
4. **Compound sentence** — two clauses each with their own subject and verb take
   a comma before *and*. One subject + two verbs takes no comma. A comma before
   *but* is fine even without a second subject.
5. **No comma splice** — never join two independent clauses with a comma; use a
   period or semicolon.
6. **Colon** — introduces a list, appositive, amplification, or quotation. If
   deleting the colon leaves a grammatical sentence of the same meaning, delete it.
7. **Opening participial phrase** — must refer to the grammatical subject.
   *"Having crashed, I rebooted the computer"* wrongly says *I* crashed.
8. **Exclamations** — use sparingly; overuse reads as shouting.
9. **Compound adjectives** — hyphenate when the first word modifies the second,
   not the noun (*best-known algorithm*). No hyphen after *-ly* adverbs or *well*
   (*well studied problem*).
10. **Parentheses + terminal punctuation** — period goes *inside* the closing
    paren only when the parenthetical is a whole standalone sentence; otherwise
    outside.
38. **Quotation punctuation (American)** — commas and periods go inside the
    quotes; colons and semicolons outside; `?` and `!` inside or outside by
    context (part of the quote → inside; part of the whole sentence → outside).

### Word choice and usage
11. **between** (two) vs. **among** (three or more).
12. **can** (ability) vs. **may** (permission).
13. **compare with** (differences between similar things) vs. **compare to**
    (similarities between different things).
14. **comprise ≠ compose** — the whole comprises the parts; never *comprised of*.
    *"The cluster comprises nodes and a network"* (not *is comprised of*).
15. **different from**, never **different than**.
16. **affect** (to influence) vs. **effect** (verb: to bring about; noun: a result).
17. **etc.** — avoid, especially after *such as* or *for example*, which already
    signal an incomplete list.
18. **farther** (physical distance) vs. **further** (time, quantity, degree).
19. **hopefully** means *with hope* — don't use it to mean *I hope* or *it is hoped*.
20. **however** — weakest at the start of a sentence; prefer mid-sentence or end.
    Fine at the start only when it means *in whatever way / to whatever extent*.
21. **fewer** (countable) vs. **less** (mass quantities).
22. **such as** (the example is a member of the set) vs. **like** (the example
    resembles but is not in the set).
23. **as** (introducing a verb/clause) vs. **like** (introducing a noun).
24. **one** — avoid as a generic person; reword instead.
25. **singular they** — Cormen prefers rewriting to avoid it (idiosyncratic; see note).
29. **Latin abbreviations** — *i.e.* = *that is* (comma after); *e.g.* =
    *for example* (comma after); *et al.* (period after *al* only). Don't begin a
    sentence with them; keep them in the surrounding font, not italics.
32. **aforementioned** — Cormen's pet peeve; avoid (idiosyncratic; see note).
33. **try to**, not **try and** — *try* takes the infinitive.
35. **while** — reserve for time/duration; use *although*, *but*, or *and* for
    contrast or addition.
36. **only** — modifies exactly the term that follows it; place it deliberately.

### Style and tone
26. **Avoid wimpy hedges** like *one of the most* / *one of the …est* — anything
    on a ranked list qualifies, so the claim says nothing.
27. **Avoid *secondly*, *thirdly*, *lastly*** — use *first, second, third … finally*.
28. **shall** (belief about the future) vs. **will** (determination) — largely
    archaic (idiosyncratic; see note).
30. **that** (restrictive, no comma) vs. **which** (nonrestrictive, set off by a
    comma). A *which* refers to the word just before its comma. Do which-hunting.
31. **this / that / these / some** are not noun phrases on their own — supply the
    noun (*This decision proved a mistake*, not bare *This proved a mistake*).
34. **very** — use sparingly; usually a sign the adjective should be stronger.
37. **Quotation marks for colloquialisms** — only when the phrase is genuinely a
    colloquialism or is ironic, never for ordinary words.

### Numbers
39. **Numbers** — spell out *zero* through *nine*; use digits for 10 and up.
    Exceptions: spell out a number that opens a sentence (or reword); use digits
    with units of measure (*3 meters*); if any number in a series needs digits,
    put them all in digits (*4, 8, and 16 nodes*); use numerals with
    *thousand/million/billion* and `$` for money; spell out ordinals *first…ninth*;
    hyphenate and spell out fractions by the same rule as integers (*one-half*).
