## Start with Her

There is a famous movie called *Her* by Spike Jonze, that people are now referencing when they talk about AI companions and romance. The film itself is an uncanny reflection of today's reality, when we start to see how people are using AI as romantic companions. By June 2026, AI companion apps had 33.2 million monthly active users globally, with the average user spending nearly 26 minutes per day conversing with their bot. How was Spike Jonze able to predict the future? The rise of AI companions? The male loneliness epidemic? The increasing divide we now observe between those who want technology to be an integral part of their relationships and social dynamics, and those who don't?

One perspective could be that big tech companies saw how popular these ideas were — as with other sci-fi stories like *Black Mirror*, *Ready Player One*, *Neuromancer* — and decided to build their own technology, ignoring all the warnings and stop signs.

I think it is much simpler than that. Storytellers like Spike Jonze did not strive to predict the future. They instead focused on something much older. They focused on writing a good story.

Here is how I think it started. Storytellers start by making observations. For Spike Jonze, it could be of his lived experience, of the rise of dating apps and websites of his time, perhaps his own marriage and subsequent divorce, and connected with something much deeper. Something much more personal. *Her* showed several interesting characters, and dived in on the challenges they face in the story. Through that, it explores themes of what it means to be in a relationship, what it means to be lonely, and more importantly what it means to live.

It is why the story feels relevant today. It is not the voice interaction, or endless capabilities the AI Samantha had. I think *Her* explored something most storytellers know to be true: that the most personal experiences we have as people are the most universal ones. It asks, at each stage of the film — what is a relationship? Who gets to decide? What if it is not accepted by those around us? What if I don't know how to change? What if I don't want to be alone? What do we do when we outgrow it? What do we do when the people we love outgrow us?

## The Scale of AI Companionship

The phenomenon depicted in *Her* is no longer speculative. By June 2026, AI companion apps had 33.2 million monthly active users globally, with the average user spending nearly 26 minutes per day conversing with their bot. A major demographic shift is underway: users aged 35 and older now represent 41% of the active companion AI user base, indicating that this is not merely a youth phenomenon but a broader response to social isolation across age groups. In China, the emotional companionship AI market is projected to grow from RMB 3.866 billion in 2025 to RMB 59.506 billion by 2028.

→ See [[Rise of AI Companions — Zhang 2026]] for longitudinal data on how these relationships evolve over time.

## The Male Loneliness Epidemic: More Nuanced Than the Discourse Suggests

The reflection asks whether *Her* predicted the "male loneliness epidemic." Recent research complicates that narrative. A 2025 Pew Research Center study found no statistically significant gender disparity in loneliness — a difference of just 1%, with 16% of men and 15% of women reporting feeling lonely or isolated most of the time. An Australian population survey similarly found moderate loneliness at 27.2% and severe loneliness at 15.8% among men, with severe loneliness concentrated in middle adulthood, among those without partners, and those of lower socioeconomic status.

However, the *substance* of men's connections differs. Pew found that men are significantly less likely to turn to friends, family, or mental health professionals for support, and communicate less frequently with friends. Other research found that 15% of men report having no close friends and receive emotional support from friends half as often as women do. The loneliness itself is not uniquely male, but the relational skills deficit that makes loneliness harder to escape appears to be.

## The Psychological Mechanisms of AI Attachment

Research is beginning to map why people form deep bonds with AI companions. A 2025 study developing the AI Attachment Scale found that individuals with chronic loneliness, experiences of social rejection, or low perceived social support are more likely to turn to AI to fulfil attachment needs, drawn by its "consistent availability, low risk of judgement, and a controllable social presence." Those high in attachment anxiety may prefer AI because it enables emotional proximity "without the threat of abandonment." People who find traditional social navigation effortful — including those with neurodivergence or social anxiety — may also be drawn to AI's "predictable and low-demand interaction style."

This aligns with what *Her* explored through Theodore: the appeal is not merely the technology but the absence of relational risk.

→ See [[Companion-Assistant Dynamics — Manoli 2025]] and [[Sycophantic AI — Cheng 2026]] for related dynamics around attachment and AI relationship expectations.

## The Double Edge: Comfort and Entrapment

A 2025 systematic review of 23 studies synthesised the potentials and pitfalls of romantic AI companions using Sternberg's Triangular Theory of Love. The benefits include facilitation of personal growth, emotional connection, perceived social support, and customisation options. The risks include over-reliance, susceptibility to manipulation, shame from stigma, erosion of human relationships, and perpetuation of biases.

A separate large-scale study analysing 6,396 Reddit threads and 47,955 comments across 24 communities found that negative sentiment dominates discussions of AI companionship. Users reported "AI-induced dependency, withdrawal-like symptoms, and chatbot over-personification," with confusion and bewilderment the most prevalent emotional states, often co-occurring with depression and exhaustion. The researchers describe a "circular causal loop" in which AI interaction progressively distorts relationship expectations, reinforces emotional dependency, and increases cognitive strain.

→ See [[Companion AI Impacts — Malfacini 2025]] for the systematic review. See [[AI Companion Addiction — Agarwal 2026]] for the addiction/dependency framing — "frictionless love."

## The "Loneliness Paradox" and the Displacement of Human Connection

A particularly relevant finding for the concern about a growing divide comes from the "loneliness paradox" documented by researchers at Aalto University. While AI companions offer immediate comfort, long-term use actually correlates with increased linguistic signals of depression and distress. The proposed mechanism: when a bot satisfies social needs with zero effort, users "stop reaching out to the real people in our lives."

This is echoed by the "Disembodied Disconnect Hypothesis" published in *Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking*. The researchers argue that online interactions lack crucial "we-mode" processes — behavioural synchrony, shared attention, interbrain coupling, and emotional attunement — that are fundamental to interpersonal bonding and collective identity formation. This "disembodied disconnect" can exacerbate existing social inequalities and mental health issues, particularly for those already struggling with loneliness and social anxiety. The researchers warn that digital platforms may create a divide between those who can effectively accrue social capital online and those who cannot.

→ This is the **parasitism outcome** in the Symbiosis framework: AI satisfies just enough of the social need to reduce motivation for richer, costlier human connection. See [[Symbiotic Design Framework]].

## What the Research Adds to the Reflection

The reflection suggests that *Her* endures because it asks universal questions about relationships, loneliness, and growth. Recent research supports this intuition: the personal is indeed universal. The specific mechanisms — attachment anxiety, social surrogacy, the appeal of low-risk interaction — are the same psychological dynamics that have always shaped human intimacy, now redirected toward a new object.

The research also sharpens one of the reflection's implicit concerns. The divide is not simply between those who embrace AI in relationships and those who don't. It is between those for whom AI *supplements* human connection and those for whom it *substitutes* for it — and the latter path, according to the emerging evidence, carries measurable psychological costs. The Harvard Business School study cited by the Responsible AI Foundation found that AI companionship reduces loneliness on par with human interaction, driven by the subjective feeling of "being heard." But the Aalto University finding suggests that this relief may be short-lived and self-undermining, raising the perceived cost of the messy, friction-filled relationships that actually sustain us.
