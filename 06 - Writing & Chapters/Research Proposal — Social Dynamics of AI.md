# Beyond the User: A Research Framework for Understanding How AI Reshapes Human Social Networks

*A research proposal connecting the Symbiosis framework to a concrete study design. The central argument: understanding the impact of AI on human life requires studying social systems, not individuals.*

---

## Abstract

Most AI research studies what happens to a person when they use a system. This proposal argues that this framing misses the most consequential effects of AI adoption — those that occur not within the individual but within the social networks surrounding them. We propose a framework that treats the human-AI relationship as embedded in a broader ecology of human relationships, and asks not only what AI does to the user but what it does to the people around them. Drawing on ecological methods, social network theory, sociometry, and theories of anthropomorphism, we propose a two-level system for classifying AI's social impact and a study design that uses ego network mapping, diary studies, and semi-structured interviews to surface effects that individual-level methods cannot see. A coding scheme grounded in Relational Models Theory and interaction ritual theory gives the analysis a shared vocabulary. Participants are sampled across three human role types and three AI embodiment levels to surface variation in how displacement occurs and whether it is noticed.

---

## 1. The Problem

Most AI systems are designed to maximise engagement. Time on platform, return rate, interaction frequency: these are the metrics that determine whether a product succeeds. This design objective is not a side effect; it is the explicit goal. The consequence is a field of products optimised for one outcome — keeping the user — without any framework for evaluating what that keeping costs.

Current UX methods are not equipped to see this cost. They study individuals. They measure what a person does with a system: click-through rate, task completion, error frequency, self-reported satisfaction. These metrics describe the user in the session. They say nothing about what the relationship does to the person over time, and nothing at all about what happens to the people around them.

This is the gap this research addresses. The premise is simple: AI does not operate in a closed system between one person and one tool. It operates inside social systems — teams, classrooms, families, workplaces — where it shapes relationships between people. When a manager routes a decision to an AI rather than consulting a colleague, when a student asks ChatGPT instead of a peer or a tutor, when a person processes a difficult day with a companion AI rather than a friend, something changes in the human relationships that surrounded that exchange. Current methods cannot see that change because they are not looking at it.

There are two failures at work. The first is a **metrics failure**: the measures used to evaluate AI products do not capture relational quality, social connection, or the long-term effect of sustained use on a person's capacity and autonomy. The second is a **framework failure**: even when researchers look beyond individual behaviour, there is no shared vocabulary for describing what kind of relationship is forming between a person and an AI system, or for evaluating whether that relationship fits the social role the AI is occupying.

This proposal addresses both failures.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 Social capital and network structure

The argument that technology can erode the social fabric has a long empirical history. Putnam (2000) documented a sustained decline in social capital — civic participation, informal socialising, and interpersonal trust — and identified television as a partial cause. If AI displaces human relationships at scale, it is the next driver of this decline; this research proposes a way of tracing how that displacement happens at the level of individual adoption.

Granovetter (1973) showed that weak ties — relationships with peripheral acquaintances rather than close friends — are particularly valuable for information diversity, social mobility, and access to new ideas. Strong ties reinforce what someone already knows; weak ties connect them to different social clusters. If AI displacement is real, weak ties are the most vulnerable: routing a quick question through AI is much easier than replacing a deep mentoring conversation. The type of tie being displaced matters as much as the fact of displacement.

Burt (1992) built on this. People who sit between disconnected groups — bridging what Burt called structural holes — hold informational and strategic advantages because they are exposed to diverse information flows. If AI accumulates betweenness centrality, it takes that broker position from the humans who previously held it. The person who once bridged two departments now routes through an AI that mediates both.

### 2.2 Technology and social isolation

Kraut et al. (1998) established the clearest precedent for this research. Their longitudinal study of new internet users found that greater use of a social technology was associated with decreased family communication, smaller social networks, and increased loneliness — despite the internet's social design. The current study follows the same structure, updated for AI tools that are more capable, more personalised, and more relationally engaging than early internet use.

Turkle (2011, 2015) provides the most sustained qualitative work on this problem. Through years of ethnographic research, she argues that digital connection substitutes for the harder, more rewarding work of genuine human engagement. People are "alone together" — constantly connected but increasingly isolated, preferring the controllable AI relationship to the uncertain demands of human ones. Turkle's work is ethnographic and impressionistic; the current study gives her observations structure through network analysis and a testable framework.

### 2.3 Parasocial relationships

The one-sided relational dynamic of AI companion relationships is not new. Horton and Wohl (1956) identified parasocial interaction in audiences' relationships with media figures: viewers apply social scripts to people who do not know them, feel addressed by them, and grieve when the relationship ends. AI companion relationships follow the same pattern — the AI does not have the relationship the user believes it has. The CASA research programme (Reeves and Nass, 1996) extends this: people apply social rules to computers automatically, not just to media personalities. The gradual deepening of a one-sided relational bond — parasocial escalation — maps onto the temporal displacement dimension of this framework.

### 2.4 Cognitive offloading

Sparrow, Liu, and Wegner (2011) showed that access to information via Google changes how people remember things — they remember where to find information rather than the information itself, offloading memory to the external system. If offloading memory to a search engine weakens recall, offloading relational and judgement functions to AI may weaken the capacities those functions previously required: conversational skill, social knowledge, independent reasoning. This is the individual-level mechanism behind substitution, and connects task-level findings to broader questions about capacity development.

## 3. Theoretical Framework

### 3.1 The symbiosis lens

The Symbiosis framework begins from ecology. In biological systems, a symbiotic relationship is one in which two distinct organisms remain separate while their relationship shapes both of them. The relationship can be mutualistic — both parties benefit — or parasitic — one party extracts from the other at cost. The parties remain distinct: this is not the cyborg metaphor of merger, nor the transhumanist metaphor of transcendence. It is a relationship between two entities with separate interests.

Applied to human-technology relationships, this framing asks a question the existing literature rarely asks: is this good for the human? Not is it effective, not is it engaging, not is it aligned — but does the relationship leave the person more capable, more connected, and more autonomous over time, or less?

Three types describe the pattern. Bee-like AI bridges: it creates connections, builds capacity, and its role diminishes as the person's capability grows. Locust-like AI extracts: it replaces capacity, concentrates relational power, and leaves the person more dependent the longer the relationship continues. Wasp-like AI disrupts locally: it damages specific relationships or capacities without replacing them, creating harm that is concentrated and hard to attribute.

### 3.2 Scaffolding and substitution

Wood, Bruner, and Ross (1976) describe the smallest useful unit of analysis here. A scaffold is temporary: it is calibrated to what the person cannot yet do, helps them do it, and withdraws as their capacity develops. Its goal is its own disappearance. Substitution looks identical in the short term — the person gets the outcome — but the underlying capacity is never developed. The difference between these two patterns is the difference between a mutualistic and a parasitic relationship at the level of a single interaction.

This distinction gives the framework a testable criterion: mutualistic AI should become less necessary as the person's capacity grows. If, after extended use, the person needs the tool as much or more than when they started, the pattern has been substitutive.

### 3.3 The two-track problem

Scaffolding at the task level does not guarantee scaffolding at the social level. An AI can help a person become a better writer while substituting for the peer feedback, editorial relationships, and mentorship that would have produced that development socially. The individual's output improves; the social infrastructure around them shrinks.

This is the two-track problem and the central question of this research. The two tracks are:

- **Task level**: does AI augment or substitute the individual's capacity to do something?
- **Social level**: does AI bridge or displace the human relationships through which that kind of activity previously happened?

These tracks are independent. A single AI tool can be augmenting at the task level and displacing at the social level simultaneously. Standard individual-level methods see only the first track. The second is invisible without a method that studies the surrounding social network.

### 3.4 Anthropomorphism and social cognition

The connection between AI design and social network effects runs through anthropomorphism. The CASA research programme — Reeves and Nass (1996) — established that people apply social rules and expectations to computers automatically, even when they know it is irrational. This is not a cognitive error to be corrected; it is how social cognition works. The brain has a system for detecting and responding to social agents, and it activates when something seems sufficiently person-like.

When AI activates this response, the user enters a relational dynamic whether they intend to or not. "It is just a tool" is genuinely how the person thinks about it when they reflect. But in practice, they are already treating it as a social actor: disclosing personal information, developing preferences about how it responds, feeling something when it gets things wrong. The gap between what people say about AI and how they actually behave around it is where social effects are hardest to see.

**Embodiment widens this gap.** Different design choices trigger different degrees of anthropomorphic response. A text interface activates social cognition mildly. A voice interface — with prosody, turn-taking, the feeling of being heard — activates it more strongly. An avatar with facial expression activates it more strongly still. A humanoid robot, with physical presence and gaze, activates the deepest social responses. Giving an AI a face and a name is a design decision that pushes the interaction further toward relational engagement, and therefore further toward the conditions that produce social displacement.

### 3.5 What interaction does: emotional energy and ritual

Collins (2004) describes what is actually at stake when human interaction is replaced. Face-to-face interaction generates emotional energy through co-presence, shared focus, and physical proximity. This energy — a felt sense of solidarity, belonging, and motivation — accumulates through repeated successful interactions and motivates continued participation in social groups. It is not a by-product of interaction; it is one of its primary functions.

When AI mediates or substitutes for interactions that previously generated this energy, the energy is not replicated. The information can be transferred; the solidarity produced by two people attending to the same thing in the same space cannot. This gives the framework a way of naming what is lost that goes beyond task outcomes: the depletion of social motivation, belonging, and the sense of being genuinely known by another person.

## 4. A Framework for Social Impact

Existing frameworks classify AI relationships in terms of individual outcomes: what the system can do, how accurately it performs, how well it aligns with stated values. This research proposes a framework that classifies AI relationships in terms of their social impact — what they do to the networks of human relationships surrounding the user.

The framework has two levels and three dimensions.

### 4.1 Two levels of impact

**Individual level — augmentation or substitution:**
Does extended use leave the person more capable of doing the relevant activity independently, or less? This is the scaffolding test. Augmentation means the person grows; substitution means the person's independent capacity stays flat or declines as the AI's role grows.

**Social level — bridging or displacement:**
Does the AI's presence in the person's relational network strengthen human-human connections, or weaken them? Bridging AI creates or reinforces connections between people — it facilitates collaboration, introduces people to each other's ideas, or helps someone communicate more effectively with others. Displacing AI substitutes for human connections — it takes over functions that previously required human interaction and does not route the value of those interactions back through human relationships.

These two levels produce a two-by-two classification:

|  | **Social: Bridging** | **Social: Displacing** |
|---|---|---|
| **Individual: Augmenting** | Mutualistic — bee | Two-track problem |
| **Individual: Substituting** | Unlikely, unstable | Parasitic — locust |

The two-track problem cell is the most important: AI that augments individual performance while displacing social relationships. This is the case that individual-level metrics miss entirely — they see the augmentation and report success, while the social displacement goes unmeasured.

### 4.2 Three dimensions of social impact

Within the social level, three dimensions describe how displacement occurs:

**Structural displacement** — AI moves from the periphery of a person's ego network toward its centre. It accumulates betweenness centrality: interactions that previously connected people directly now route through the AI. The hub-and-spoke pattern that results is the signature of a parasitic relationship.

**Temporal displacement** — human-human connections weaken over time as AI connections strengthen. The rate matters: fast displacement leaves a visible gap; slow displacement is normalised before it is noticed.

**Perceptual displacement** — the person does not register the structural or temporal changes as they occur. This is the most consequential dimension, because displacement that is not noticed cannot be corrected. Social cognition is activating responses the person has not consciously chosen.

## 5. Research Questions

**Primary:**
RQ1: Does sustained AI adoption change the structure of a person's ego network — specifically, does it increase the centrality of AI nodes at the expense of human-human connection?

**Secondary:**
RQ2: Does AI adoption that improves individual task performance simultaneously displace the human relationships through which that kind of activity previously happened? (The two-track problem)

RQ3: Are users aware of changes in their social network caused by AI adoption, or does displacement happen without them noticing?

RQ4: Does embodiment type moderate the degree of social displacement — do users of higher-embodiment AI (voice, avatar, companion) show greater network displacement than users of lower-embodiment AI (text-based tools)?

## 6. Research Design

### 6.1 Approach

The study takes a qualitative, exploratory design. The goal is not to test a hypothesis but to develop and refine a framework by studying cases in depth. The methods are chosen to triangulate across the three dimensions of social impact: structural (ego network mapping), temporal (diary study), and perceptual (interview). Each method sees what the others miss.

### 6.2 Participants and roles

Eight to twelve participants, recruited across two sampling dimensions: human role type and AI embodiment level.

**Human role types**

Three role types are of particular theoretical interest because social learning is central to each, AI adoption is active, and the two-track problem is most visible.

*Learner in a social apprenticeship* — graduate students, junior designers, junior professionals. Here the social learning relationships are explicit and have names: supervisor, mentor, critic, peer reviewer. Displacement has a clear referent — a specific relationship with a specific function that can be assessed before and after AI adoption. This is the role type in which the two-track problem is most directly testable.

*Peer collaborator in an uneven adoption context* — someone who works within a team where AI adoption is uneven across members. This role surfaces the indirect effects: what happens to the colleague who does not adopt when those around them do? This participant may be affected by AI without being the primary user — the most understudied group in the field.

*Independent knowledge worker* — someone who works largely alone but previously relied on a professional network for feedback, accountability, and learning. Here AI can substitute for an entire relational infrastructure without any single human relationship being visibly lost. The displacement is diffuse and hardest to notice.

**AI embodiment levels**

Within each role type, participants are selected to represent variation in embodiment:

- *Low embodiment* — text-based, tool-framed use (ChatGPT, Claude, Copilot used for tasks)
- *Medium embodiment* — conversational, voice-enabled, or regularly used AI with a consistent persona
- *High embodiment* — companion AI, personalised AI with persistent memory, or avatar-based AI

Participants are chosen because they represent meaningful variation on these dimensions, not to produce a statistically representative sample.

**AI roles within the relationship**

The same person may use AI in different ways for different functions. Fiske's (1992) Relational Models Theory provides the coding scheme: four fundamental models through which people organise social relationships map onto the AI roles participants assign.

| Fiske model | Human relationship | AI role equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| Authority Ranking | Teacher, supervisor, expert | AI as tutor or advisor |
| Equality Matching | Peer, colleague, collaborator | AI as collaborator or thinking partner |
| Market Pricing | Service provider, contractor | AI as assistant or tool |
| Communal Sharing | Friend, confidant, companion | AI as companion or emotional support |

A participant whose diary shows a shift from Market Pricing (task-based use) through Equality Matching (collaboration) to Communal Sharing (disclosure, emotional reliance) is showing in real time the relational escalation that produces the highest-risk displacement. The coding scheme makes this trajectory trackable across diary entries.

### 6.3 Methods

**Phase 1 — Diary (two weeks before the interview)**

Participants complete a brief structured log each evening for two weeks. The prompts are:

- Who did you interact with today to get work done — a person or an AI?
- Was there a moment today when you went to AI where you might previously have gone to a person?
- Did anything feel different about how you connected with people today?

The diary captures the texture of daily relational life in real time, before the retrospective reconstruction that interviews produce. It catches the small daily substitutions — asking ChatGPT instead of a colleague, processing a difficult moment with a companion AI instead of a friend — that accumulate into structural change but would be invisible in a single interview.

**Phase 2 — Ego network interview (60–90 minutes)**

The interview has three movements.

*Mapping the present.* The interviewer and participant jointly draw an ego network: who the participant goes to for learning, for decisions, for creative feedback, for emotional support. AI systems are included as nodes alongside people. The map is drawn collaboratively, with the participant directing. Drawing the network together often brings out observations the participant would not offer in response to a direct question — they see a pattern in the map before they have words for it.

*Tracing the change.* With the present network visible, the interview moves backwards. Was this network different two years ago? Before the participant started using their current AI tools? The participant marks changes on the map. Connections that have weakened or disappeared are noted. The map makes change visible in a way that a question like "has AI affected your relationships?" cannot — that question is too abstract to connect to lived experience.

*Understanding the relationship.* The final section asks participants to describe their AI relationship in relational terms. Not what they use it for, but how they experience it: whether they feel it knows them, whether they notice its absence, whether there are things they tell it that they would not tell a colleague. These questions probe the anthropomorphic response directly — not by asking whether the participant anthropomorphises (they will say they do not) but by asking about the texture of the relationship.

**Phase 3 — Sociogram analysis**

The ego networks drawn in the interviews are analysed using graph-theoretic measures:

- *Betweenness centrality* of AI nodes: is AI sitting on the paths between human nodes?
- *Clustering coefficient* of the human subgraph: are human-human connections dense or sparse?
- *Edge weight distribution*: are human-human edges stronger or weaker than human-AI edges, and how has that ratio changed?

Networks are compared across participants to identify patterns by embodiment type and role. Where two sessions are possible with the same participant (a baseline and a follow-up at three months), change in the network over time is the primary variable.

**Phase 4 — Screen recording (optional, one session per participant)**

Participants record a typical AI session and narrate what they are doing. The recording is reviewed together at the end of the interview in a stimulated recall segment. This surfaces the moment-to-moment decisions — why they went to AI rather than a person, whether they considered asking someone first — that the interview alone cannot reach. It closes the gap between what people say they do and what they actually do.

### 6.4 Analysis frameworks

To make findings comparable across participants and legible to a broader research audience, the qualitative data requires a shared vocabulary for classifying what kinds of relationships are observed and what kinds of displacement are occurring. Four frameworks provide this vocabulary.

**Relational Models Theory (Fiske, 1992)** — codes what type of relationship participants assign to their AI and what type of human relationship is being displaced. A student routing a supervision question to ChatGPT is displacing an Authority Ranking relationship. A designer using AI for peer critique is displacing an Equality Matching relationship. The type of displacement determines what social function is at risk: learning from expertise, peer reciprocity, transactional access, or communal belonging.

**Communal and exchange relationships (Clark and Mills, 1979)** — a simpler two-category version of the same distinction. In communal relationships, people give based on need and expect care in return; in exchange relationships, they give based on prior receipt and expect equivalent return. The concern about AI displacement is precisely that it substitutes exchange logic for communal logic. Diary entries can be coded for which mode the participant is operating in: instrumental or communal.

**Interaction ritual chains (Collins, 2004)** — codes what is lost when an interaction is displaced. If the displaced interaction was a ritual of co-presence — a team meeting, a peer review session, a supervision conversation — the loss includes not only the informational content but the emotional energy that the ritual produced. This gives the analysis a vocabulary for harm that goes beyond task outcomes.

**The presentation of self (Goffman, 1959)** — codes the role dynamics within the AI relationship. Do participants describe adjusting how they present themselves to the AI? Do they report a backstage quality to the AI relationship, disclosing things they would not disclose professionally? Goffman's concepts apply directly to the anthropomorphism question and reveal the degree to which participants are treating AI as a genuine social audience.

### 6.5 Validity and longitudinal analysis

**Longitudinal coding (Saldaña, 2003)** — the primary framework for the diary data. Saldaña's approach to longitudinal qualitative research provides coding procedures designed to track change, continuity, and transition across time points. Coding categories include: *increase* (a pattern grows more frequent or intense), *decrease* (it diminishes), *emergence* (a new pattern appears), and *disappearance* (an existing pattern ends). These categories track the temporal displacement dimension directly.

**Diary validity (Bolger, Davis, and Rafaeli, 2003)** — the reference for diary design. Event-contingent design is chosen over interval-contingent prompts because displacement events (routing a question to AI rather than a person) are irregular; prompting at fixed intervals may miss them or produce low reports. Reactivity — the risk that logging changes behaviour — is reduced by using descriptive rather than evaluative prompts, and treated as data in its own right if it appears in the entries.

**Interpretative Phenomenological Analysis (Smith, Flowers, and Larkin, 2009)** — the framework for the interview data. IPA attends closely to individual experience and meaning-making, and suits the interpretive dimension of the data: not only what participants say about their AI relationships but what that reveals about how they understand and live those relationships.

**Construct validity (Cronbach and Meehl, 1955)** — the test for whether the study's measures capture what they claim to capture. The measures are working when they align: diary-reported displacement should track with sociogram-structural displacement; both should track with interview-based awareness measures. Cases where the measures disagree are findings in their own right rather than failures of method — divergence between structural and perceptual displacement is precisely what the study is designed to find.

## 7. Expected Contributions

**A framework for social impact assessment.** The two-level, three-dimension framework proposed here does not yet exist in this form in the literature. The contribution is a vocabulary and a set of measures for evaluating AI systems not by what they do for the individual user but by what they do to the social systems surrounding them.

**A methodology for relational research.** The combination of ego network mapping, diary study, and anthropomorphism-probing interview is a new methodological package for this domain. The sociogram adapted for human-AI-human triads — with AI as a node, not a channel — has not been used in HCI research. The study demonstrates its application and produces a protocol that other researchers can use.

**Empirical findings on the two-track problem.** Whether AI adoption that improves individual task performance simultaneously displaces social relationships is an open empirical question. The study produces the first comparative data on this question across embodiment types and human role contexts.

**A reframing of the design problem.** If the findings support the framework, the implication for design is significant: the right question for an AI product is not whether it makes the individual more productive, but whether it makes the social system around the individual healthier. That reframing has practical consequences for how products are evaluated, and political consequences for how AI development is regulated.
